# Review: The Message (Ar-Risalah)

## Summary
While the production value and cinematography are top-notch—especially for its time—the narrative often feels contradictory or illogical when analyzed closely. The film functions well as a piece of cinema, but heavily romanticizes or simplifies complex historical and theological realities.

## The Good: Production & Atmosphere
* **Cinematography:** The visual storytelling is excellent.
* **Production Value:** High-quality sets and costumes.
* **Music/Score:** Captures the epic nature of the events effectively.

## Critical Analysis & "Mughalatat" (Inaccuracies)

### 1. The Portrayal of Quraysh
* **Nuanced Villains:** Contrary to typical "evil" tropes, the movie initially shows Quraysh as relatively tolerant. They allowed Muhammad to speak his mind and proselytize (*Da'wah*) without immediate consequence.
* **Civilization vs. Chaos:** From a modern perspective, the early Muslims shouting *Allahu Akbar* in the streets appeared uncivilized and disturbing to the public peace. It makes sense why the Quraysh leaders found this disruptive.

### 2. Psychology of Belief
* **The "Poor" Demographic:** The film accurately shows that the first followers were largely the poor and enslaved (e.g., Bilal).
* **Motivation:** There is a psychological aspect to this—people with nothing to lose are more likely to follow someone promising them status and eternal rewards.
* **The Promise of Jannah:** The descriptions of rivers of milk and wine serve as a powerful incentive for starving people. (Note: The movie conveniently omits the "women/Hoor al-ayn" aspect of Jannah to appeal to a wider audience).

### 3. Logic vs. Faith
* **Martyrdom:** The stubbornness of followers under torture (refusing to fake apostasy to save their lives) seems illogical. A pragmatic deity would likely prefer them to stay alive and worship in secret.
* **Divine Intervention:** Scenes like the rock on Bilal are powerful symbols, but rely on "divine logic" rather than cause-and-effect.

### 4. Contradictions in Violence
* **Defensive vs. Offensive:** The movie heavily emphasizes the Quranic command: *"Don't fight unless they fight you."*
    * *Critique:* This contradicts later Islamic history where Muslims initiated conquests (e.g., the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople/Istanbul) for power, treasure, and expansion, not just self-defense.
* **Fear Tactics:** Both sides utilize fear. The Muslims used the strength and intimidation factor of Hamza to gain freedom and followers, mirroring the tactics of their oppressors.
* **Convenience of Revelation:** The shift from "no fighting" to "permission to fight" (Surah Al-Hajj 22:39) felt like a convenient plot device to solve the protagonists' problems rather than a divine mandate.

### 5. Historical Accuracy: Women's Rights
* **The Claim:** The movie (and modern apologists) claim Islam brought equality to women (*"A'azza al-Islamu al-Mar'ah"*).
* **The Reality:** Historic texts (Hadith/Quran) suggest otherwise, such as the ruling that one man's testimony equals that of two women, or differences in inheritance. The movie paints a modernized picture of equality that conflicts with historical jurisprudence.

## Fact Checks & Notes

### Q: The Fighting Verse
* **Context:** The verse mentioned in the film is **Surah Al-Hajj (22:39)**: *"Permission [to fight] has been given to those who are being fought, because they were wronged."*
* **Timeline:** This is historically accepted as the first verse revealed that permitted armed struggle, marking the shift from the pacifist Meccan phase to the militant Medinan phase.
