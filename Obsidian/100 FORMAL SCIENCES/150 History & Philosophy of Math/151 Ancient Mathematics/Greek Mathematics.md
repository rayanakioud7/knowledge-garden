# Greek Mathematics — The Development of Pure Mathematics

> [!summary] Core Idea
> Greek mathematics began as a **practical craft** used in trade, calculation, surveying, and engineering, but its major historical achievement was transforming mathematics into a **theoretical discipline** based on **general principles** and **formal proof**.

![[placeholder_greco_roman_mathematicians_map.png]]
*Suggested image: map/timeline of major Greco-Roman mathematicians, from **Thales of Miletus (~600 BCE)** to **Hypatia of Alexandria (~400 CE)**.*

## 1. Overview

The Greeks divided mathematics into two main branches:

- **Arithmetic** → study of **discrete quantity** ("multitude")
- **Geometry** → study of **continuous quantity** ("magnitude")

Ancient writers claimed these came from practical needs:

- **Geometry** from **Egyptian land surveying**, especially after Nile floods changed property boundaries
- **Arithmetic** from **Phoenician commerce and trade**

These reports come mainly from **Proclus** (5th c. CE), but likely reflect earlier traditions known to **Herodotus** and **Eudemus**.

> [!info] Important Distinction
> Greek mathematics was **not originally unique** in its practical uses. Its originality lay in turning mathematical knowledge into a **deductive, proof-based science**.

![[placeholder_arithmetic_vs_geometry.png]]
*Diagram: The Greek division — Arithmetic (discrete) vs. Geometry (continuous)*

> [!info] Etymology
> *Geometria* literally means **"measurement of land"** — reflecting its practical surveying origins.

## 2. Practical Roots of Early Greek Mathematics

Evidence for early Greek mathematics is limited, but it shows strong practical use.

### Main examples :

| Area              | Evidence / Use                                                                     |
| ----------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Numeration**    | Greeks used a numeral system similar in principle to Roman numerals                |
| **Computation**   | The **abacus** was used by Greeks and Egyptians                                    |
| **Surveying**     | City planners used geometric rules for land measurement                            |
| **Approximation** | Used $( \frac{7}{5} = 1.4 )$ as an estimate for $( \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\ldots )$ |
| **Engineering**   | **Eupalinus of Megara** built an aqueduct tunnel through a mountain on Samos       |
| **Education**     | Plato praised Egyptian training in practical arithmetic and geometry               |

### Example: Approximation of $( \sqrt{2} )$

Greek surveyors treated a square of side \(50\) plethra as having diagonal \(70\) plethra:

$$
\text{Estimated ratio} = \frac{70}{50} = \frac{7}{5} = 1.4
$$

But in reality:

$$
\sqrt{2} \approx 1.4142\ldots
$$

So this was a useful **practical approximation**, even if not exact.

> [!note]
> Later Greek practical mathematics, such as the papyri from **Ptolemaic Egypt** and the manuals of **Heron of Alexandria**, resembles earlier Egyptian and Mesopotamian traditions. The Greeks likely **borrowed** many practical techniques from these older civilizations.

## 3. What Made Greek Mathematics Unique

The real Greek breakthrough was the creation of mathematics as a **theoretical science**.

### This meant two major changes:
1. **Generality** — results were stated as universal truths, not just recipes for specific cases  
2. **Proof** — results had to be logically demonstrated

> [!important] Greek Innovation
> Earlier cultures often had correct procedures and numerical examples.  
> The Greeks asked: **Why is this always true?**  
> That question led to **proof**, **axiomatic structure**, and **pure mathematics**.


![[placeholder_practical_vs_theoretical_math.png]]
*Diagram: The shift from practical procedure to theoretical proof*

---
### Case Study: Pythagorean Triples

> [!example] From Procedure to Proof
> 
> **Mesopotamian approach** — found specific sets by procedure:
> $$(3, 4, 5) \qquad (5, 12, 13) \qquad (119, 120, 169)$$
> 
> **Greek approach** — proved a **general rule** for generating **all** such triples:
> 
> For two whole numbers $p$ and $q$ (both even or both odd, with $pq$ a perfect square):
> 
> $$a = \frac{p - q}{2}, \qquad b = \sqrt{pq}, \qquad c = \frac{p + q}{2}$$
> 
> **Euclid proved** these satisfy:
> $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2$$
> *(Elements, Book X)*
> 
> Furthermore, while Mesopotamians **understood** these form sides of right triangles, the Greeks **proved** it:
> - *Elements*, Book I, Proposition 47
> - *Elements*, Book VI, Proposition 31 (more general form)

> [!important] Why This Matters
> Mesopotamian mathematics could *generate* useful numerical solutions.  
> Greek mathematics aimed to *prove* why such solutions work and how they fit into a wider geometric theory.

## 5. The Pre-Euclidean Transition

The move from practical to theoretical mathematics happened **before Euclid**, probably in the **5th century BCE**.

### Key figures

| ------------------------ | --------------- | ---------------------------------------------- |

| [[Hippocrates of Chios]] | Late 5th c. BCE | Early organization of geometry into “elements” |

| [[Theaetetus of Athens]] | 4th c. BCE      | Important work on irrational magnitudes        |

## 6. The Crisis of Irrational Numbers

One of the most important turning points in Greek mathematics was the discovery of **incommensurable magnitudes**.

### The Pythagorean Assumption

The early Pythagoreans held:

> *"All things are number"*

This implied every geometric magnitude could be expressed as a ratio of whole numbers (a **rational number**). In Greek, *arithmos* referred exclusively to whole numbers or ordinary fractions.

### The Breakdown

For a **unit square** (side = 1):

$$\text{diagonal} = \sqrt{2}$$

> [!fail] Proof by Contradiction
> Assume $\sqrt{2} = \frac{a}{b}$ where $a, b$ are whole numbers with no common factors.
> 
> $$2 = \frac{a^2}{b^2} \implies a^2 = 2b^2$$
> 
> Therefore $a^2$ is even → $a$ is even → let $a = 2k$:
> 
> $$4k^2 = 2b^2 \implies b^2 = 2k^2$$
> 
> Therefore $b$ is also even.
> 
> **Contradiction!** — Both $a$ and $b$ cannot be even if they share no common factors.
> 
> $$\therefore \sqrt{2} \text{ is irrational}$$


![[placeholder_unit_square_diagonal_sqrt2.png]]
*Diagram: Unit square with side = 1 and diagonal = $\sqrt{2}$, showing incommensurability*

> [!important] Why This Was Revolutionary
> Early Pythagoreans believed that “all things are number,” meaning magnitudes could be expressed through whole numbers or fractions.  
> The discovery of irrationals showed that **arithmetic alone was not enough** for geometry.


### Expansion of the Theory

| Date | Development |
|---|---|
| 5th c. BCE | $\sqrt{2}$ shown incommensurable (possibly within Pythagorean school) |
| ~400 BCE | $\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{5}$, and other roots shown incommensurable |
| 4th c. BCE | **Theaetetus** generalized: $\sqrt{p}$ is irrational whenever $p$ is not a perfect square |
| 4th c. BCE | **Eudoxus** contributed further to theory of irrationals |
| ~300 BCE | Results compiled into **Book X of Euclid's *Elements*** (115 propositions) |

![[placeholder_number_line_irrationals.png]]

These results were later developed into a sophisticated theory, especially in **Book X of Euclid’s _Elements_**.

### Greek Terminology

| Modern Term           | Greek Concept                                     |     |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- | --- |
| **Irrational number** | Lengths that cannot be expressed as $\frac{a}{b}$ |     |
| **Incommensurable**   | No common unit measures both lengths              |     |
| **Rational number**   | Expressible as ratio of whole numbers             |     |
> [!info] Key Distinction
> The Greeks did **not** call $\sqrt{2}$ a "number" — they called such pairs of lengths **"incommensurable"** (*asymmetros*). The modern usage of "irrational number" extends the concept of number itself.

## 7. Why Irrationals Changed Mathematics

> [!important] Why This Mattered So Deeply
> 
> 1. **Arithmetic is insufficient for geometry** — not all lengths can be captured by whole-number ratios
> 2. **"Obvious" assumptions can be false** — if commensurability fails, what else might?
> 3. **All mathematical claims need justification** — nothing can be taken for granted
> 4. **The concept of "proof" itself needs definition** — what reasoning qualifies as rigorous?

This drove the creation of:
- Early **"elements" textbooks** by Hippocrates of Chios and others (5th c. BCE)
- Euclid's definitive ***Elements*** (~300 BCE)

## 8. Euclid and the Systematization of Mathematics

Around **300 BCE**, [[Euclid of Alexandria]] brought together earlier work in the **_Elements_**, the most influential mathematical text of antiquity.

### Why the *Elements* mattered
- organized geometry into a **deductive sequence**
- started from definitions, common notions, and propositions
- gave mathematics a **systematic structure**
- preserved and unified work from earlier thinkers

> [!summary] Euclid's Role
> Euclid did not create Greek mathematics from nothing; he **organized, refined, and systematized** the achievements of earlier mathematicians.

## 9. Philosophical Background

Greek mathematics developed inside a broader culture of intense philosophical debate.

| Thinker / School             | Period         | Main Idea                                | Relevance to Mathematics                          |
| ---------------------------- | -------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------- |
| [[Parmenides]]               | 5th c. BCE     | Only what is permanent truly exists      | Raised questions about knowledge and certainty    |
| [[Heracleitus]]              | ~500 BCE       | Everything is in flux                    | Challenged permanence and fixed truth             |
| [[Zeno of Elea]]             | 6th–4th c. BCE | Paradoxes of motion and division         | Exposed difficulties with infinity and continuity |
| [[Democritus]] / Atomists    | 5th c. BCE     | Reality made of indivisible atoms        | Difficult to reconcile with geometric continuity  |
| [[Protagoras]]               | 5th c. BCE     | Raised geometric puzzles                 | Questioned exactness in geometry                  |
| [[Antiphon]] & [[Bryson]]    | 5th c. BCE     | Compared circles with inscribed polygons | Early thinking related to limits                  |
| [[Pythagoreans]] / [[Plato]] | 5th c. BCE     | Mathematics as a model of certainty      | Elevated math as ideal knowledge                  |

### Main philosophical issues
- the **infinitely large** and **infinitely small**
- **continuity** vs. indivisible units
- the relation between **geometry** and **physical reality**
- the meaning of **proof**, **truth**, and **existence**

> [!note]
> Greek mathematicians were not isolated technicians. They worked in an environment where even the meanings of **knowledge** and **proof** were under debate.

### Zeno's Paradox of Infinite Division

> [!example] The Paradox
> Assume a line can be bisected **again and again without limit**:
> 
> - If the division ultimately yields **points of zero length** → even infinitely many sum to **zero**
> - If it yields **tiny segments** → their sum is **infinite**
> 
> $$\text{Length} = \begin{cases} 0 + 0 + 0 + \cdots = 0 \\ \epsilon + \epsilon + \epsilon + \cdots = \infty \end{cases}$$
> 
> The line's length would have to be **both zero and infinite** — a contradiction.

### The Atomist Response

**Democritus** proposed indivisible **atoms** (*atomon* = "uncuttable") as a solution. But this created a **new problem** for geometry:

> [!fail] Conflict
> If atoms are the smallest units, they would serve as a **universal measure** for all lines — including incommensurable ones. But incommensurable lines **cannot** share a common unit. Contradiction.

## 10. Historical Importance

Greek mathematics stands apart from earlier traditions because it emphasized:

- **general theory**
- **logical proof**
- **careful treatment of assumptions**
- **systematic organization of knowledge**

Even if the exact causes of this transformation cannot be fully reconstructed, its importance is clear:

> [!important] Historical Significance
> Greek mathematics became the model of **pure mathematics** — a discipline concerned not only with correct answers, but with **demonstrating why those answers must be true**.

## Final Takeaway

> [!summary] The Greek Mathematical Revolution
> Greek mathematics **started** as practical computation and measurement — inherited from Egypt and Mesopotamia. Its **revolution** was the move to **proof-based, general, theoretical reasoning**.
> 
> The discovery of **irrational magnitudes** ($\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt{3}$, $\sqrt{5}$, …) shattered the assumption that "all things are number" and forced a fundamental rethinking:
> - What is a valid **proof**?
> - Which **assumptions** can be trusted?
> - How should mathematical knowledge be **organized**?
> 
> Combined with a uniquely **competitive philosophical culture** (Parmenides, Zeno, Democritus, Plato), these pressures transformed mathematics into a **deductive, axiomatic discipline** — a transformation whose effects endure to this day.
> 
> $$\boxed{\text{Practical craft} \xrightarrow{\text{irrationals + philosophy}} \text{Theoretical science}}$$
