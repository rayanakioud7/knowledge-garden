## The Derivative as a Function

By shifting perspective from a fixed number $a$ to a variable $x$, the derivative can be regarded as a new function derived from the original function $f$ through a limiting operation. 

> [!info] Definition: The Derivative Function
> The **derivative of $f$** is defined by the formula:
> $$f'(x) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h}$$
> The domain of $f'$ is the set $\{x \mid f'(x) \text{ exists}\}$, which may be smaller than the domain of $f$.

Geometrically, $f'(x)$ corresponds to the slope of the tangent line to the graph of $f$ at the point $(x, f(x))$.

---

### Examples

> [!example] Example 1: Graphical Estimation of $f'$
> The graph of a function $f$ is given in Figure (a). Use it to sketch the graph of the derivative $f'$.
> 
> **Solution:**
> The value of the derivative at any given $x$ is estimated by finding the slope of the tangent line at $(x, f(x))$:
> 
> * **Horizontal Tangents:** At the local extrema $A$, $B$, and $C$, the tangent lines are horizontal, meaning the slope is $0$. Therefore, the derivative graph crosses the $x$-axis ($f'(x) = 0$) at the corresponding points $A'$, $B'$, and $C'$ directly beneath them.
> * **Positive Slope:** On the intervals between $A$ and $B$, and to the right of $C$, the tangent lines have a positive slope, meaning $f'(x) > 0$. For instance, at $x = 5$, the slope is approximately $\frac{3}{2}$, so $f'(5) \approx 1.5$, which plots the point $P'(5, 1.5)$.
> * **Negative Slope:** On the intervals to the left of $A$ and between $B$ and $C$, the tangent lines have a negative slope, meaning $f'(x) < 0$.
>

### 1. Polynomial and Radical Derivatives

Calculation of the derivative function $f'(x)$ treating $x$ as a constant relative to the limit variable $h$.

---

### Examples

> [!example] Example 2: Cubic Polynomial Derivative
> (a) If $f(x) = x^3 - x$, find a formula for $f'(x)$.
> (b) Illustrate by comparing the graphs of $f$ and $f'$.
> 
> **Solution:**
> (a) Using the definition of the derivative function:
> $$\begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{[(x + h)^3 - (x + h)] - [x^3 - x]}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{x^3 + 3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3 - x - h - x^3 + x}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{3x^2h + 3xh^2 + h^3 - h}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} (3x^2 + 3xh + h^2 - 1) \\
> &= 3x^2 - 1
> \end{aligned}$$
> 
> (b) Comparing the graphs in (Figure 3), $f'(x) = 0$ where $f$ has horizontal tangents, and $f'(x) > 0$ when the tangents of $f$ have a positive slope.

> [!example] Example 3: Square Root Function Derivative
> If $f(x) = \sqrt{x}$, find the derivative of $f$ and state the domain of $f'$.
> 
> **Solution:**
> Rationalize the numerator using the conjugate expression:
> $$\begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x)}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{x + h} - \sqrt{x}}{h} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \left( \frac{\sqrt{x + h} - \sqrt{x}}{h} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x}}{\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x}} \right) \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x + h) - x}{h(\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x})} \\
> &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{x + h} + \sqrt{x}} \\
> &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{x} + \sqrt{x}} = \frac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}
> \end{aligned}$$
> 
> The derivative $f'(x)$ exists if $x > 0$, so the domain of $f'$ is $(0, \infty)$. This is smaller than the domain of $f$, which is $[0, \infty)$.

## Other Notations

### 1. Alternative Derivative Notations

For $y = f(x)$, the derivative can be written as:

$$
f'(x) = y' = \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{df}{dx} = \frac{d}{dx}f(x) = Df(x) = D_x f(x)
$$

- $D$ and $\dfrac{d}{dx}$ are called **differentiation operators** — they denote the operation of **differentiation** (computing a derivative).
- $dy/dx$ (Leibniz notation) is **not** a ratio; it is a synonym for $f'(x)$.

---

### 2. Leibniz Form of the Derivative Definition

> [!NOTE]
> 
> $$
> \frac{dy}{dx} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x}
> $$
> 

---

### 3. Evaluating at a Point

To denote the derivative at a specific number $a$:

$$
\left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right|_{x=a} \qquad \text{or} \qquad \left.\frac{dy}{dx}\right]_{x=a}
$$

Both are synonyms for $f'(a)$.

---

> [!info] Definition 3 — Differentiability
> A function $f$ is **differentiable at $a$** if $f'(a)$ exists.
>
> It is **differentiable on an open interval** $(a,b)$ [or $(a,\infty)$ or $(-\infty,a)$ or $(-\infty,\infty)$] if it is differentiable at every number in the interval.

---

### Example 5: Differentiability of $f(x) = |x|$

**Problem:** Where is the function $f(x) = |x|$ differentiable?

**Solution:**

**Case 1: $x > 0$**

If $x > 0$, then $|x| = x$, and for $h$ small enough, $x + h > 0$, so $|x+h| = x+h$. Therefore:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|x+h| - |x|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(x+h) - x}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{h}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} 1 = 1
\end{aligned}
$$

So $f$ is differentiable for any $x > 0$.

**Case 2: $x < 0$**

If $x < 0$, then $|x| = -x$, and for $h$ small enough, $x + h < 0$, so $|x+h| = -(x+h)$. Therefore:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|x+h| - |x|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-(x+h) - (-x)}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-h}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (-1) = -1
\end{aligned}
$$

So $f$ is differentiable for any $x < 0$.

**Case 3: $x = 0$**

Investigate:

$$
f'(0) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(0+h) - f(0)}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{|0+h| - |0|}{h} \quad \text{(if it exists)}
$$

Compute left and right limits separately:

$$
\lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|0+h| - |0|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{|h|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^+} \frac{h}{h} = 1
$$

$$
\lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{|0+h| - |0|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{|h|}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0^-} \frac{-h}{h} = -1
$$

Since these one-sided limits differ, $f'(0)$ **does not exist**.

---

**Conclusion:** $f$ is differentiable at all $x \neq 0$, with

$$
f'(x) = \begin{cases} 1 & \text{if } x > 0 \\ -1 & \text{if } x < 0 \end{cases}
$$

> [!warning] Geometric Interpretation
> The non-existence of $f'(0)$ reflects the fact that $y = |x|$ has **no tangent line** at $(0,0)$ — the graph has a sharp corner there.

## Theorem 4: Differentiability Implies Continuity

> [!info] Theorem 4
> If $f$ is differentiable at $a$, then $f$ is continuous at $a$.

---

### Proof

**Goal:** Show $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$, i.e., $f(x) - f(a) \to 0$ as $x \to a$.

**Given:** $f$ is differentiable at $a$, so

$$
f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}
$$

exists. Divide and multiply $f(x) - f(a)$ by $x - a$ (valid for $x \neq a$):

$$
f(x) - f(a) = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}\,(x - a)
$$

Applying the Product Law:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to a} \left[ f(x) - f(a) \right] &= \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}\,(x - a) \\
&= \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a} \cdot \lim_{x \to a}(x - a) \\
&= f'(a) \cdot 0 = 0
\end{aligned}
$$

Now add and subtract $f(a)$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{x \to a} f(x) &= \lim_{x \to a} \left[ f(a) + (f(x) - f(a)) \right] \\
&= \lim_{x \to a} f(a) + \lim_{x \to a} \left[ f(x) - f(a) \right] \\
&= f(a) + 0 = f(a)
\end{aligned}
$$

Therefore $f$ is continuous at $a$. $\blacksquare$

---

> [!warning] Note: Converse is False
> **Continuous $\not\Rightarrow$ Differentiable.** For example, $f(x) = |x|$ is continuous at $0$ since
>
> $$\lim_{x \to 0} f(x) = \lim_{x \to 0} |x| = 0 = f(0)$$
>
> but $f$ is **not differentiable** at $0$ (shown in Example 5).

---

## How Can a Function Fail to Be Differentiable?

By the contrapositive of Theorem 4: if $f$ is not continuous at $a$, then $f$ is not differentiable at $a$.

There are **three** ways a function can fail to be differentiable at a point $x = a$:

---

### 1. A Corner or Kink

- The graph changes direction abruptly at $x = a$.
- The left and right limits of the difference quotient (i.e., one-sided derivatives) are **different**, so $f'(a)$ does not exist.
- Example: $y = |x|$ at $x = 0$.

![[Pasted image 20260704131709.png|center]]

---

### 2. A Discontinuity

- $f$ is not continuous at $a$ (e.g., a jump discontinuity).
- By Theorem 4 (contrapositive), $f$ fails to be differentiable.

---

### 3. A Vertical Tangent Line

- $f$ is continuous at $a$, but the tangent line is vertical:

$$
\lim_{x \to a} |f'(x)| = \infty
$$

- The tangent lines become steeper and steeper as $x \to a$.

---

![[Pasted image 20260704131731.png]]

---

### Zooming Interpretation of Differentiability

- If $f$ **is** differentiable at $a$: zooming in toward $(a, f(a))$ causes the graph to straighten out and appear more like a line.
- If $f$ **is not** differentiable (corner/kink): no amount of zooming eliminates the sharp point.

## Higher Derivatives

### 1. The Second Derivative

If $f$ is differentiable, then $f'$ is itself a function which may have its own derivative, denoted $(f')' = f''$, called the **second derivative** of $f$.

**Leibniz notation** for $y = f(x)$:

$$
\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right) = \frac{d^2 y}{dx^2}
$$

---

### Example 6: Second Derivative of $f(x) = x^3 - x$

**Problem:** If $f(x) = x^3 - x$, find and interpret $f''(x)$.

**Solution:** From Example 2, $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 1$. Then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
f''(x) = (f')'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f'(x+h) - f'(x)}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{[3(x+h)^2 - 1] - [3x^2 - 1]}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{3x^2 + 6xh + 3h^2 - 1 - 3x^2 + 1}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} (6x + 3h) = 6x
\end{aligned}
$$

**Interpretation:** $f''(x)$ is the slope of the curve $y = f'(x)$ at the point $(x, f'(x))$ — i.e., the rate of change of the slope of the original curve $y = f(x)$.

> [!info] Sign Check
> $f''(x)$ is **negative** where $y = f'(x)$ has negative slope, and **positive** where $y = f'(x)$ has positive slope.

---

### 2. The Third Derivative

$$
f''' = (f'')'
$$

Interpreted as the slope of $y = f''(x)$, or the rate of change of $f''(x)$.

Alternative notations for $y = f(x)$:

$$
y''' = f'''(x) = \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{d^2 y}{dx^2}\right) = \frac{d^3 y}{dx^3}
$$

---

### 3. The $n$th Derivative

The fourth derivative is denoted $f^{(4)}$. In general, the $n$th derivative is obtained by differentiating $f$ a total of $n$ times:

$$
y^{(n)} = f^{(n)}(x) = \frac{d^n y}{dx^n}
$$

---

### Example 7: Third and Fourth Derivatives of $f(x) = x^3 - x$

**Problem:** If $f(x) = x^3 - x$, find $f'''(x)$ and $f^{(4)}(x)$.

**Solution:** From Example 6, $f''(x) = 6x$. The graph $y = 6x$ is a straight line with slope $6$, so:

$$
f'''(x) = 6 \quad \text{for all } x
$$

Since $f'''$ is a constant function, its graph is a horizontal line, hence:

$$
f^{(4)}(x) = 0 \quad \text{for all } x
$$
