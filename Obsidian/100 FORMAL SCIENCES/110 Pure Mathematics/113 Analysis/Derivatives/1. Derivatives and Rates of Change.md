## Derivatives and Rates of Change

### 1. Tangents
If a curve $C$ has equation $y = f(x)$ and we want to find the tangent line to $C$ at the point $P(a, f(a))$, consider a nearby point $Q(x, f(x))$, where $x \neq a$. The slope of the secant line $PQ$ is given by:

$$m_{PQ} = \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}$$

As $Q$ approaches $P$ along the curve $C$ by letting $x$ approach $a$, if $m_{PQ}$ approaches a number $m$, then the tangent $t$ is defined to be the line through $P$ with slope $m$. This amounts to saying that the tangent line is the limiting position of the secant line $PQ$ as $Q$ approaches $P$.

![[Pasted image 20260703234708.png|center]]

> [!info] Definition 1: Tangent Line (First Form)
> The **tangent line** to the curve $y = f(x)$ at the point $P(a, f(a))$ is the line through $P$ with slope
> $$m = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}$$
> provided that this limit exists.

---

### 2. Alternative Expression for Slope
An alternative expression for the slope of a tangent line uses the substitution $h = x - a$, which implies $x = a + h$. The slope of the secant line $PQ$ becomes:

$$m_{PQ} = \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$$

As $x$ approaches $a$, $h$ approaches $0$, allowing the tangent line slope definition to be rewritten.
 
![[Pasted image 20260703234749.png|center]]

> [!info] Definition 2: Tangent Line (Second Form)
> The slope of the tangent line to the curve $y = f(x)$ at the point $P(a, f(a))$ can be expressed as:
> $$m = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$$

---

### Example 1: Tangent to a Parabola
Find an equation of the tangent line to the parabola $y = x^2$ at the point $P(1, 1)$.

**Solution:**
Here, $a = 1$ and $f(x) = x^2$. The slope $m$ is calculated as:

$$\begin{aligned}
m &= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{f(x) - f(1)}{x - 1} \\
&= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} \\
&= \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1} \\
&= \lim_{x \to 1} (x + 1) = 1 + 1 = 2
\end{aligned}$$

Using the point-slope form of the equation of a line ($y - y_1 = m(x - x_1)$), the equation of the tangent line at $(1, 1)$ is:

$$\begin{aligned}
y - 1 &= 2(x - 1) \\
y &= 2x - 1
\end{aligned}$$

### Example 2: Tangent to a Hyperbola
Find an equation of the tangent line to the hyperbola $y = 3/x$ at the point $(3, 1)$.

**Solution:**
Let $f(x) = 3/x$. The slope of the tangent at $(3, 1)$ using the second definition form is:

$$\begin{aligned}
m &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(3 + h) - f(3)}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\dfrac{3}{3 + h} - 1}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\dfrac{3 - (3 + h)}{3 + h}}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{-h}{h(3 + h)} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} -\frac{1}{3 + h} = -\frac{1}{3}
\end{aligned}$$

Therefore, an equation of the tangent at the point $(3, 1)$ is:

$$y - 1 = -\frac{1}{3}(x - 3)$$

which simplifies to:

$$x + 3y - 6 = 0$$

### 3. Derivatives
The limit defining the instantaneous rate of change or the slope of the tangent line is formally named the derivative of a function at a point.

> [!info] Definition 4: The Derivative at a Number $a$
> The **derivative of a function $f$ at a number $a$**, denoted by $f'(a)$ (read as "$f$ prime of $a$"), is defined as:
> $$f'(a) = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h}$$
> provided that this limit exists.

---

### 4. Alternative Form of the Derivative
By substituting $x = a + h$, it follows that $h = x - a$. As $h \to 0$, $x \to a$, yielding an equivalent formulation for the derivative:

> [!info] Definition 5: Alternative Derivative Formulation
> $$f'(a) = \lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x) - f(a)}{x - a}$$

---

### Example 4: Calculating the Derivative at an Arbitrary Value
Find the derivative of the function $f(x) = x^2 - 8x + 9$ at the number $a$.

**Solution:**
Applying Definition 4:

$$\begin{aligned}
f'(a) &= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(a + h) - f(a)}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{[(a + h)^2 - 8(a + h) + 9] - [a^2 - 8a + 9]}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{a^2 + 2ah + h^2 - 8a - 8h + 9 - a^2 + 8a - 9}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{2ah + h^2 - 8h}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0} (2a + h - 8) \\
&= 2a - 8
\end{aligned}$$

---

## Geometric Interpretation of the Derivative

The derivative $f'(a)$ represents the slope of the tangent line to the curve $y = f(x)$ at the point $(a, f(a))$.

> [!info] Tangent Line Formula
> The tangent line to $y = f(x)$ at $(a, f(a))$ is:
> $$y - f(a) = f'(a)(x - a)$$

---


#### Example 5: Tangent Line Equation
Find an equation of the tangent line to the parabola $y = x^2 - 8x + 9$ at the point $(3, -6)$.

**Solution:**
The derivative of $f(x) = x^2 - 8x + 9$ at a general value $a$ is given as $f'(a) = 2a - 8$. 

1. **Compute the slope at $a = 3$:**
   $$f'(3) = 2(3) - 8 = -2$$

2. **Substitute into point-slope equation:**
   $$\begin{aligned}
   y - (-6) &= -2(x - 3) \\
   y + 6 &= -2x + 6 \\
   y &= -2x
   \end{aligned}$$

## Rates of Change

For a quantity $y$ that depends on another quantity $x$ through a function $y = f(x)$, let $x$ change from $x_1$ to $x_2$. 

* **Increment of $x$:**
  $$\Delta x = x_2 - x_1$$
* **Corresponding change in $y$:**
  $$\Delta y = f(x_2) - f(x_1)$$

### 1. Average vs. Instantaneous Rates of Change

The difference quotient defines the **average rate of change** of $y$ with respect to $x$ over the interval $[x_1, x_2]$, which geometrically represents the slope of the secant line $PQ$:
$$\frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1}$$

![[Pasted image 20260704001853.png|center|417]]

By letting $x_2$ approach $x_1$ (which forces $\Delta x$ to approach $0$), we obtain the **instantaneous rate of change**.

> [!info] Equation 6: Instantaneous Rate of Change
> The instantaneous rate of change of $y$ with respect to $x$ at $x = x_1$ is defined as the limit of the average rates of change:
> $$\text{instantaneous rate of change} = \lim_{\Delta x \to 0} \frac{\Delta y}{\Delta x} = \lim_{x_2 \to x_1} \frac{f(x_2) - f(x_1)}{x_2 - x_1}$$

---

### 2. Derivative Interpretation

The limit for the instantaneous rate of change is equivalent to the definition of the derivative $f'(x_1)$.

> [!info] Rate of Change Interpretation of the Derivative
> The derivative $f'(a)$ is the instantaneous rate of change of $y = f(x)$ with respect to $x$ when $x = a$.
