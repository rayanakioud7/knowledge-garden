## Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

### Table: Derivatives of Trigonometric Functions

> [!info] Summary of Trig Derivatives
>
> | Function | Derivative | Function | Derivative |
> |---|---|---|---|
> | $\sin x$ | $\cos x$ | $\csc x$ | $-\csc x \cot x$ |
> | $\cos x$ | $-\sin x$ | $\sec x$ | $\sec x \tan x$ |
> | $\tan x$ | $\sec^2 x$ | $\cot x$ | $-\csc^2 x$ |

> [!tip] Memorization Aid
> The **minus signs** appear on the derivatives of the **"cofunctions"** — cosine, cosecant, and cotangent.

### 2. Two Key Trigonometric Limits

> [!info] Fundamental Trig Limits
>
> $$\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1 \tag{2}$$
>
> $$\lim_{\theta \to 0} \frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta} = 0 \tag{3}$$

---

#### Proof of Equation 2 (via Squeeze Theorem)

Assume $0 < \theta < \pi/2$. Consider a circular sector of radius $1$ with central angle $\theta$ centered at $O$, with $BC \perp OA$.

- By definition of radian measure: $\text{arc } AB = \theta$.
- $|BC| = |OB|\sin\theta = \sin\theta$.

**Lower bound:** From the diagram, $|BC| < |AB| < \text{arc } AB$, so

$$
\sin\theta < \theta \implies \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} < 1
$$

**Upper bound:** Let tangent lines at $A$ and $B$ meet at $E$. Since the arc is shorter than the circumscribed polygonal path:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\theta = \text{arc } AB &< |AE| + |EB| \\
&< |AE| + |ED| = |AD| = |OA|\tan\theta = \tan\theta
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $\theta < \dfrac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta}$, i.e.,

$$
\cos\theta < \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} < 1
$$

Since $\lim_{\theta \to 0} \cos\theta = 1$ and $\lim_{\theta \to 0} 1 = 1$, the **Squeeze Theorem** gives

$$
\boxed{\lim_{\theta \to 0^+} \frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = 1}
$$

Since $(\sin\theta)/\theta$ is even, the two-sided limit equals $1$. $\blacksquare$

#### Proof of Equation 3

Multiply numerator and denominator by $\cos\theta + 1$:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta} &= \lim_{\theta \to 0}\left(\frac{\cos\theta - 1}{\theta} \cdot \frac{\cos\theta + 1}{\cos\theta + 1}\right) = \lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\cos^2\theta - 1}{\theta(\cos\theta + 1)} \\
&= \lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{-\sin^2\theta}{\theta(\cos\theta + 1)} = -\lim_{\theta \to 0}\left(\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \cdot \frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta + 1}\right) \\
&= -\lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} \cdot \lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\cos\theta + 1} \\
&= -1 \cdot \frac{0}{1 + 1} = 0 \quad \text{(by Eq. 2)}
\end{aligned}
$$

### 3. Derivative of $\sin x$

Apply the definition and the sine addition formula $\sin(x+h) = \sin x \cos h + \cos x \sin h$:

$$
\begin{align}
f'(x) &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin(x+h) - \sin x}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin x \cos h + \cos x \sin h - \sin x}{h} \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0}\left[\frac{\sin x \cos h - \sin x}{h} + \frac{\cos x \sin h}{h}\right] \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0}\left[\sin x\left(\frac{\cos h - 1}{h}\right) + \cos x\left(\frac{\sin h}{h}\right)\right] \\
&= \lim_{h \to 0}\sin x \cdot \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\cos h - 1}{h} + \lim_{h \to 0}\cos x \cdot \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{\sin h}{h} \tag{1}
\end{align}
$$

Using $\lim_{h \to 0}\sin x = \sin x$, $\lim_{h \to 0}\cos x = \cos x$, together with $(2)$ and $(3)$:

$$
f'(x) = (\sin x)(0) + (\cos x)(1) = \cos x
$$

> [!info] Derivative of Sine
> $$\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) = \cos x \tag{4}$$

> [!example] Example 1: Product Rule with $\sin x$
> 
> **Problem:** Differentiate $y = x^2 \sin x$.
> 
> **Solution:** By the Product Rule and Formula (4):
> 
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \frac{dy}{dx} &= x^2\,\frac{d}{dx}(\sin x) + \sin x\,\frac{d}{dx}(x^2) \\
> &= x^2 \cos x + 2x \sin x
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>

## Derivatives of Cosine and Tangent

### 1. Derivative of $\cos x$

Using the same techniques as in the proof of Formula (4):

> [!info] Derivative of Cosine
> $$\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x \tag{5}$$

---

### 2. Derivative of $\tan x$

Apply the Quotient Rule to $\tan x = \dfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$, using Formulas (4) and (5):

$$
\begin{aligned}
\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) &= \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\sin x}{\cos x}\right) \\
&= \frac{\cos x \dfrac{d}{dx}(\sin x) - \sin x \dfrac{d}{dx}(\cos x)}{\cos^2 x} \\
&= \frac{\cos x \cdot \cos x - \sin x(-\sin x)}{\cos^2 x} \\
&= \frac{\cos^2 x + \sin^2 x}{\cos^2 x} \\
&= \frac{1}{\cos^2 x} = \sec^2 x
\end{aligned}
$$

> [!info] Derivative of Tangent
> $$\frac{d}{dx}(\tan x) = \sec^2 x \tag{6}$$

## Examples: Trigonometric Derivatives and Limits

> [!example] Example 2: Horizontal Tangents of a Trig Quotient
> **Problem:** Differentiate $f(x) = \dfrac{\sec x}{1 + \tan x}$. For what values of $x$ does $f$ have a horizontal tangent?
>
> **Solution:** By the Quotient Rule,
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> f'(x) &= \frac{(1 + \tan x)\dfrac{d}{dx}(\sec x) - \sec x \dfrac{d}{dx}(1 + \tan x)}{(1 + \tan x)^2} \\
> &= \frac{(1 + \tan x)\sec x \tan x - \sec x \cdot \sec^2 x}{(1 + \tan x)^2} \\
> &= \frac{\sec x(\tan x + \tan^2 x - \sec^2 x)}{(1 + \tan x)^2} \\
> &= \frac{\sec x(\tan x - 1)}{(1 + \tan x)^2}
> \end{aligned}
> $$
>
> **Simplification identity used:** $\tan^2 x + 1 = \sec^2 x$.
>
> Since $\sec x \neq 0$, $f'(x) = 0$ iff $\tan x = 1$, i.e.,
>
> $$x = n\pi + \frac{\pi}{4}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}$$

> [!example] Example 5: Limit Involving $\sin(7x)$
> **Problem:** Find $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 7x}{4x}$.
>
> **Note:** $\sin 7x \neq 7 \sin x$.
>
> **Solution:** Multiply and divide by $7$ to match Equation (2):
>
> $$
> \frac{\sin 7x}{4x} = \frac{7}{4}\left(\frac{\sin 7x}{7x}\right)
> $$
>
> Let $\theta = 7x$; then $\theta \to 0$ as $x \to 0$:
>
> $$
> \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\sin 7x}{4x} = \frac{7}{4}\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sin 7x}{7x} = \frac{7}{4}\lim_{\theta \to 0}\frac{\sin\theta}{\theta} = \frac{7}{4} \cdot 1 = \frac{7}{4}
> $$

---

> [!example] Example 6: Limit of $x \cot x$
> **Problem:** Calculate $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} x \cot x$.
>
> **Solution:** Rewrite and divide numerator/denominator by $x$:
>
> $$
> \begin{aligned}
> \lim_{x \to 0} x \cot x &= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{x \cos x}{\sin x} \\
> &= \lim_{x \to 0} \frac{\cos x}{\dfrac{\sin x}{x}} = \frac{\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \cos x}{\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 0} \dfrac{\sin x}{x}} \\
> &= \frac{\cos 0}{1} \quad \text{(by continuity of cosine and Equation 2)} \\
> &= 1
> \end{aligned}
> $$
