## 1.6 Calculating Limits Using the Limit Laws

### Core Limit Laws

Suppose that $c$ is a constant and the individual limits $\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x)$ exist.

> [!info] Operational Theorems
> 1. **Sum Law:** The limit of a sum is the sum of the limits.
>    $$\lim_{x \to a} [f(x) + g(x)] = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) + \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$$
> 2. **Difference Law:** The limit of a difference is the difference of the limits.
>    $$\lim_{x \to a} [f(x) - g(x)] = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) - \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$$
> 3. **Constant Multiple Law:** The limit of a constant times a function is the constant times the limit of the function.
>    $$\lim_{x \to a} [c f(x)] = c \lim_{x \to a} f(x)$$
> 4. **Product Law:** The limit of a product is the product of the limits.
>    $$\lim_{x \to a} [f(x)g(x)] = \lim_{x \to a} f(x) \cdot \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$$
> 5. **Quotient Law:** The limit of a quotient is the quotient of the limits, provided the divisor limit is non-zero.
>    $$\lim_{x \to a} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \frac{\lim_{x \to a} f(x)}{\lim_{x \to a} g(x)} \quad \text{if} \quad \lim_{x \to a} g(x) \neq 0$$

> [!example] Example 1: Evaluating Limits Graphically
> Using the curves of $f$ and $g$ given in a coordinate grid, evaluate the expressions:
> 	![[Pasted image 20260625225629.png|center]]
> **(a) $\lim_{x \to -2} [f(x) + 5g(x)]$**
> From the graphs, extract the directional height targets at $x = -2$:
> $$\lim_{x \to -2} f(x) = 1 \qquad \lim_{x \to -2} g(x) = -1$$
> Apply the Sum and Constant Multiple Laws:
> $$\lim_{x \to -2} f(x) + 5 \lim_{x \to -2} g(x) = 1 + 5(-1) = -4$$
> 
> **(b) $\lim_{x \to 1} [f(x)g(x)]$**
> - From the graph, $\lim_{x \to 1} f(x) = 2$.
> - For $g(x)$, a jump discontinuity occurs at $x = 1$: the left limit is $-2$ and the right limit is $-1$. Because $\lim_{x \to 1^-} g(x) \neq \lim_{x \to 1^+} g(x)$, the two-sided limit $\lim_{x \to 1} g(x)$ **does not exist**.
> - **Conclusion:** The Product Law cannot be applied directly for a two-sided limit. Since one-sided limits yield unequal products ($2 \cdot (-2) = -4$ vs $2 \cdot (-1) = -2$), the overall limit **does not exist (DNE)**.
> 
> **(c) $\lim_{x \to 2} \frac{f(x)}{g(x)}$**
> From the graph, $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) \approx 1.4$ and $\lim_{x \to 2} g(x) = 0$.
> - **Conclusion:** Because the limit of the denominator is $0$, we cannot invoke the Quotient Law. The limit **does not exist** (the value diverges as the denominator approaches zero while the numerator approaches a non-zero number).

### Power and Root Extensions

By applying the core operational properties repeatedly, we get additional foundational limits where $n$ is a positive integer:

> [!info] Specialized Power & Root Theorems
> 6. **Power Law:** $\lim_{x \to a} [f(x)]^n = \left[\lim_{x \to a} f(x)\right]^n$
> 7. **Constant Identity:** $\lim_{x \to a} c = c$
> 8. **Linear Identity:** $\lim_{x \to a} x = a$
> 9. **Monomial Power Law:** $\lim_{x \to a} x^n = a^n$
> 10. **Radical Identity:** $\lim_{x \to a} \sqrt[n]{x} = \sqrt[n]{a} \quad$ *(If $n$ is even, assume $a > 0$)*
> 11. **General Root Law:** $\lim_{x \to a} \sqrt[n]{f(x)} = \sqrt[n]{\lim_{x \to a} f(x)} \quad$ *(If $n$ is even, assume $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) > 0$)*

> [!example] Example 2: Analytic Proof using Step-by-Step Laws
> Evaluate the limits and justify each algebraic transition step:
> 
> **(a) $\lim_{x \to 5} (2x^2 - 3x + 4)$**
> $$\begin{aligned}
> \lim_{x \to 5} (2x^2 - 3x + 4) &= \lim_{x \to 5} (2x^2) - \lim_{x \to 5} (3x) + \lim_{x \to 5} 4 && \text{(Laws 1, 2)} \\
> &= 2 \lim_{x \to 5} x^2 - 3 \lim_{x \to 5} x + \lim_{x \to 5} 4 && \text{(Law 3)} \\
> &= 2(5^2) - 3(5) + 4 && \text{(Laws 7, 8, 9)} \\
> &= 50 - 15 + 4 = 39
> \end{aligned}$$
> 
> **(b) $\lim_{x \to -2} \frac{x^3 + 2x^2 - 1}{5 - 3x}$**
> Since the denominator limit is non-zero ($5 - 3(-2) = 11 \neq 0$), apply the Quotient Law:
> $$\begin{aligned}
> \lim_{x \to -2} \frac{x^3 + 2x^2 - 1}{5 - 3x} &= \frac{\lim_{x \to -2} (x^3 + 2x^2 - 1)}{\lim_{x \to -2} (5 - 3x)} && \text{(Law 5)} \\
> &= \frac{\lim_{x \to -2} x^3 + 2 \lim_{x \to -2} x^2 - \lim_{x \to -2} 1}{\lim_{x \to -2} 5 - 3 \lim_{x \to -2} x} && \text{(Laws 1, 2, 3)} \\
> &= \frac{(-2)^3 + 2(-2)^2 - 1}{5 - 3(-2)} && \text{(Laws 7, 8, 9)} \\
> &= \frac{-8 + 8 - 1}{5 + 6} = -\frac{1}{11}
> \end{aligned}$$

## The Direct Substitution Property

Notice from Example 2 that the calculated value matches the value obtained by directly substituting the target value into the expression ($f(5) = 39$). This special behavior applies to all smooth, continuous function forms.

> [!info] The Direct Substitution Property
> If $f$ is a polynomial or a rational function, and $a$ is within the valid algebraic domain of $f$, then:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$$

## Algebraic Techniques for Calculating Limits

When direct substitution fails because a function is undefined at $x=a$ (often producing an indeterminate form $\frac{0}{0}$), preliminary algebra can be used to simplify the expression.

### 1. Factoring Method
> [!info] Fundamental Principle
> If $f(x) = g(x)$ when $x \neq a$, then $\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$, provided the limit exists. Since limits evaluate the behavior *near* $a$ and not *at* $a$, common factors can be canceled.

* **Example:** Find $\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1}$.
  Factoring the numerator as a difference of squares:
  $$\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x^2 - 1}{x - 1} = \lim_{x \to 1} \frac{(x - 1)(x + 1)}{x - 1} = \lim_{x \to 1} (x + 1) = 1 + 1 = 2$$

![[Pasted image 20260625232024.png|center]]

### 2. Expansion Method
* **Example:** Evaluate $\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(3 + h)^2 - 9}{h}$.
  Expand the numerator algebraically before taking the limit:
  $$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{(9 + 6h + h^2) - 9}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{6h + h^2}{h} = \lim_{h \to 0} (6 + h) = 6 + 0 = 6$$

### 3. Rationalization Method
When radical expressions cause an indeterminate form, multiply the numerator and denominator by the **conjugate** expression.

* **Example:** Find $\lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} - 3}{t^2}$.
  Multiply by the conjugate $\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3$:
  $$\begin{aligned}
  \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} - 3}{t^2} \cdot \frac{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3}{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3} &= \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{(t^2 + 9) - 9}{t^2(\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3)} \\
  &= \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{t^2}{t^2(\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3)} \\
  &= \lim_{t \to 0} \frac{1}{\sqrt{t^2 + 9} + 3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{0 + 9} + 3} = \frac{1}{6}
  \end{aligned}$$

## One-Sided Limit Properties & Piecewise Functions

> [!important] Existence Theorem Reminder
> A two-sided limit exists if and only if both the left-hand and right-hand limits exist and are equal:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \iff \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$$

* **Example (Absolute Value Function):** Prove $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{|x|}{x}$ does not exist.
  Evaluate directional definitions of $|x|$ near $0$:
  - **Right-hand ($x > 0 \implies |x| = x$):** $\lim_{x \to 0^+} \frac{x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0^+} 1 = 1$
  - **Left-hand ($x < 0 \implies |x| = -x$):** $\lim_{x \to 0^-} \frac{-x}{x} = \lim_{x \to 0^-} (-1) = -1$
  Since $1 \neq -1$, the limit **does not exist (DNE)**.

![[Pasted image 20260625232112.png|center]]

* **Example (Greatest Integer Function $\lfloor x \rfloor$):** Show $\lim_{x \to 3} \lfloor x \rfloor$ does not exist.
  The greatest integer function returns the largest integer less than or equal to $x$.
  - $\lim_{x \to 3^+} \lfloor x \rfloor = \lim_{x \to 3^+} 3 = 3 \quad (\text{for } 3 \le x < 4)$
  - $\lim_{x \to 3^-} \lfloor x \rfloor = \lim_{x \to 3^-} 2 = 2 \quad (\text{for } 2 \le x < 3)$
  Since the one-sided limits are unequal, the limit does not exist.

## The Squeeze Theorem

### Order Property of Limits
If $f(x) \le g(x)$ when $x$ is near $a$ (except possibly at $a$) and the limits of $f$ and $g$ both exist as $x \to a$, then:
$$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) \le \lim_{x \to a} g(x)$$

### The Squeeze Theorem (Sandwich Theorem)
> [!info] Theorem Formulation
> If $f(x) \le g(x) \le h(x)$ when $x$ is near $a$ (except possibly at $a$) and:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \lim_{x \to a} h(x) = L$$
> Then:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = L$$

> [!example] Example 11: Applying the Squeeze Theorem
> Show that $\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = 0$.
> 
> *Note: The Product Law cannot be used here because $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin(1/x)$ does not exist due to infinite oscillation.*
> 
> **Proof Step-by-Step:**
> 1. Start with the geometric bounds of the standard sine wave:
>    $$-1 \le \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \le 1$$
> 2. Multiply all parts of the inequality by the positive value $x^2$ ($x^2 \ge 0$ for all $x$):
>    $$-x^2 \le x^2 \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) \le x^2$$
> 3. Evaluate the limits of the bounding outer functions using direct substitution:
>    $$\lim_{x \to 0} (-x^2) = 0 \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to 0} x^2 = 0$$
> 4. By the Squeeze Theorem, the middle function must converge to the same value:
>    $$\lim_{x \to 0} x^2 \sin\left(\frac{1}{x}\right) = 0$$
