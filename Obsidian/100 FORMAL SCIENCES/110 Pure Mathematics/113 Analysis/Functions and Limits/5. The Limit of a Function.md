## The Limit of a Function

### Intuitive Definition

Suppose a function $f(x)$ is defined when $x$ is near a number $a$ (meaning $f$ is defined on an open interval containing $a$, except possibly at $a$ itself). 

> [!info] Notation & Definition
> We write:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \quad \text{or} \quad f(x) \to L \;\text{ as }\; x \to a$$
> and say **"the limit of $f(x)$, as $x$ approaches $a$, equals $L$."**
> 
> This means we can make the values of $f(x)$ arbitrarily close to $L$ by taking $x$ to be sufficiently close to $a$ (on either side of $a$) but **not equal to $a$** ($x \neq a$).


### Crucial Distinction: $f(a) \neq \lim_{x \to a} f(x)$
When finding the limit of $f(x)$ as $x \to a$, we **never** consider $x = a$. The function $f(x)$ does not even need to be defined at $x = a$. The only thing that matters is how $f$ behaves *near* $a$.

> [!example] Example 1: Rational Function hole
> Guess the value of $\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x - 1}{x^2 - 1}$.
> 
> **Numerical Analysis:**
> The function $f(x) = \frac{x-1}{x^2-1}$ is undefined at $x = 1$. Let's evaluate inputs surrounding $1$:
> 
> | $x < 1$ | $f(x)$ | | $x > 1$ | $f(x)$ |
> | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
> | 0.5 | 0.666667 | | 1.5 | 0.400000 |
> | 0.9 | 0.526316 | | 1.1 | 0.476190 |
> | 0.99 | 0.502513 | | 1.01 | 0.497512 |
> | 0.999 | 0.500250 | | 1.001 | 0.499750 |
> | 0.9999 | 0.500025 | | 1.0001 | 0.499975 |
> 
> As $x$ approaches $1$ from both sides, $f(x)$ approaches $0.5$. Thus, we guess:
> $$\lim_{x \to 1} \frac{x - 1}{x^2 - 1} = 0.5$$

## Pitfalls in Guessing Limits Numerically

Relying purely on calculator evaluations or graphing utilities can lead to incorrect conclusions due to scale problems or precision limits.

### 1. Finite Calculator Precision Error
Evaluating $\lim_{t \to 0} \frac{\sqrt{t^2+9}-3}{t^2}$ yields a theoretical limit of $\frac{1}{6} \approx 0.166667$. However, evaluating values of $t$ extremely close to $0$ on a standard calculator eventually yields an output of $0$. This occurs because the calculator rounds $\sqrt{t^2+9}$ to $3.000\dots$, producing a false value.

![[Pasted image 20260625205732.png]]

### 2. High-Frequency Oscillations
Investigate $\lim_{x \to 0} \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{x}\right)$.
Evaluating at integer patterns yields zero outputs every time: $f(1) = \sin\pi = 0$, $f(0.1) = \sin 10\pi = 0$, $f(0.01) = \sin 100\pi = 0$. This mistakenly suggests the limit is $0$. 
However, the function oscillates between $1$ and $-1$ infinitely often as $x \to 0$. Because the values do not approach a single fixed number, **the limit does not exist (DNE)**.

## One-Sided Limits

To look at directional approaches independently, we use one-sided limit structures.

### The Heaviside Step Function
The Heaviside function $H(t)$ is defined as:
$$H(t) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } t < 0 \\ 1 & \text{if } t \ge 0 \end{cases}$$
As $t \to 0$ from the left, $H(t) \to 0$. As $t \to 0$ from the right, $H(t) \to 1$. Because these directional behaviors do not match, the two-sided limit $\lim_{t \to 0} H(t)$ does not exist.

---

> [!info] Left-Hand and Right-Hand Limit Formulations
> * **Left-Hand Limit (Approaching from the Left):**
>   $$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L$$
>   We restrict $x$ to values **less than $a$** ($x < a$).
> * **Right-Hand Limit (Approaching from the Right):**
>   $$\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$$
>   We restrict $x$ to values **greater than $a$** ($x > a$).

![[Pasted image 20260625205829.png|center]]

### Two-Sided Limit Existence Theorem
> [!info] Theorem
> The two-sided limit exists if and only if both individual one-sided limits exist and are exactly equal to each other:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = L \quad \iff \quad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = L \quad \text{and} \quad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = L$$

---

> [!example] Example 7: Piecewise Graphical Evaluation
> Given the piecewise curve graph of $g(x)$ below, determine the explicit limit values:
> 
> **1. Behavior at $x = 2$:**
> - Left-hand: $\lim_{x \to 2^-} g(x) = 3$
> - Right-hand: $\lim_{x \to 2^+} g(x) = 1$
> - **Conclusion:** $\lim_{x \to 2} g(x)$ **does not exist** because the one-sided limits are unequal ($3 \neq 1$).
> 
> **2. Behavior at $x = 5$:**
> - Left-hand: $\lim_{x \to 5^-} g(x) = 2$
> - Right-hand: $\lim_{x \to 5^+} g(x) = 2$
> - **Conclusion:** $\lim_{x \to 5} g(x) = 2$. (Note that even though $\lim_{x \to 5} g(x) = 2$, the actual point value is detached: $g(5) = 1$).

## Infinite Limits

Consider the behavior of the function $f(x) = \frac{1}{x^2}$ as $x$ approaches $0$. As $x$ becomes close to $0$, $x^2$ also becomes close to $0$, causing $1/x^2$ to become exceptionally large.

| $x$ | $\frac{1}{x^2}$ |
| :--- | :--- |
| $\pm 1$ | $1$ |
| $\pm 0.5$ | $4$ |
| $\pm 0.2$ | $25$ |
| $\pm 0.1$ | $100$ |
| $\pm 0.05$ | $400$ |
| $\pm 0.01$ | $10,000$ |
| $\pm 0.001$ | $1,000,000$ |

The outputs do not settle on a single real number; instead, they grow without bound. To describe this particular mode of divergence, we use the notation:
$$\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x^2} = \infty$$

![[Pasted image 20260625223100.png|center]]

---

> [!info] Definition: Positive Infinite Limits
> Let $f$ be a function defined on both sides of $a$, except possibly at $a$ itself. Then
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \infty$$
> means that the values of $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily large (as large as we please) by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $a$, but not equal to $a$.

An alternative notation for this behavior is:
$$f(x) \to \infty \quad \text{as} \quad x \to a$$

![[Pasted image 20260625223236.png|center]]

---

> [!info] Definition: Negative Infinite Limits
> Let $f$ be defined on both sides of $a$, except possibly at $a$ itself. Then
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = -\infty$$
> means that the values of $f(x)$ can be made arbitrarily large negative by taking $x$ sufficiently close to $a$, but not equal to $a$.

![[Pasted image 20260625223308.png|center]]

### One-Sided Infinite Limits
Infinite behaviors can also be analyzed from directional approaches independently:
$$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \infty \qquad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \infty$$
$$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = -\infty \qquad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = -\infty$$

## Vertical Asymptotes

> [!info] Definition
> The line $x = a$ is called a **vertical asymptote** of the curve $y = f(x)$ if at least one of the following statements is true:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = \infty \qquad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = \infty \qquad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = \infty$$
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = -\infty \qquad \lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = -\infty \qquad \lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = -\infty$$

---

> [!example] Example 9: Evaluating Infinite Limits for a Rational Function
> Find $\lim_{x \to 3^+} \frac{2x}{x - 3}$ and $\lim_{x \to 3^-} \frac{2x}{x - 3}$.
> 
> **Step-by-Step Analysis:**
> 
> **1. Right-hand approach ($x \to 3^+$):**
> - If $x$ is close to $3$ but slightly larger than $3$, the denominator $x - 3$ is a very small **positive** number.
> - The numerator $2x$ approaches $6$.
> - A positive number divided by a positive vanishing value yields a positive unbounded output:
>   $$\lim_{x \to 3^+} \frac{2x}{x - 3} = \infty$$
> 
> **2. Left-hand approach ($x \to 3^-$):**
> - If $x$ is close to $3$ but slightly smaller than $3$, the denominator $x - 3$ is a very small **negative** number.
> - The numerator $2x$ still approaches the positive value $6$.
> - A positive number divided by a negative vanishing value yields a negative unbounded output:
>   $$\lim_{x \to 3^-} \frac{2x}{x - 3} = -\infty$$
> 
> **Conclusion:** The line $x = 3$ is a vertical asymptote for the function.

![[Pasted image 20260625223601.png|center]]

---

> [!example] Example 10: Finding Vertical Asymptotes of Trigonometric Functions
> Find the vertical asymptotes of $f(x) = \tan x$.
> 
> **Solution:**
> Express the function using its fundamental quotient identity:
> $$\tan x = \frac{\sin x}{\cos x}$$
> Potential vertical asymptotes occur where the denominator is zero ($\cos x = 0$). This condition is satisfied at odd integer multiples of $\pi/2$:
> $$x = \frac{(2n + 1)\pi}{2} \quad \text{where } n \in \mathbb{Z}$$
> 
> Testing near $x = \pi/2$:
> - As $x \to (\pi/2)^-$, $\cos x \to 0^+$ and $\sin x > 0 \implies \lim_{x \to (\pi/2)^-} \tan x = \infty$
> - As $x \to (\pi/2)^+$, $\cos x \to 0^-$ and $\sin x > 0 \implies \lim_{x \to (\pi/2)^+} \tan x = -\infty$
> 
> Thus, the lines $x = (2n + 1)\pi/2$ are all vertical asymptotes of the curve.
