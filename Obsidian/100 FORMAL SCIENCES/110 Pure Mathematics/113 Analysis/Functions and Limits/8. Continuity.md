## Continuity

A continuous process is one that takes place gradually, without interruption or abrupt change. Geometrically, you can think of a continuous function as a curve that can be drawn without lifting your pen from the paper.

### Definition of Continuity

> [!info] Definition: Continuity at a Point
> A function $f$ is **continuous at a number $a$** if:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$$

Notice that this definition implicitly requires **three conditions** to be satisfied simultaneously:
1. **$f(a)$ is defined** (i.e., $a$ is in the domain of $f$).
2. **$\lim_{x \to a} f(x)$ exists**.
3. **$\lim_{x \to a} f(x) = f(a)$** (the limit matches the point value).

If $f$ is defined near $a$ but is not continuous at $a$, we say that $f$ is **discontinuous at $a$** (or has a **discontinuity** at $a$).

---

> [!example] Example 1: Identifying Discontinuities Graphically
> Look at the discontinuous graph below to pinpoint exactly where and why $f$ breaks:
> - **At $a = 1$:** Discontinuous because $f(1)$ is not defined (open circle hole).
> - **At $a = 3$:** Discontinuous because $\lim_{x \to 3} f(x)$ does not exist (the left and right limits are different heights).
> - **At $a = 5$:** Discontinuous because even though $f(5)$ is defined and the limit exists, they are unequal: $\lim_{x \to 5} f(x) \neq f(5)$.

### Classification of Discontinuities

Discontinuities typically fall into three clear categories, illustrated by the examples below:

1. **Removable Discontinuity:** The curve has a localized hole that can be "redefined" or repaired by filling in a single point value.
2. **Infinite Discontinuity:** The function shoots to positive or negative infinity on one or both sides, creating a vertical asymptote.
3. **Jump Discontinuity:** The function steps abruptly from one height level to another, causing the one-sided limits to be unequal ($Heaviside$).

---

> [!example] Example 2: Classifying Analytical Discontinuities
> Where are each of the following functions discontinuous?
> 
> **(a) $f(x) = \frac{x^2 - x - 2}{x - 2}$**
> - **Analysis:** $f(2)$ is not defined because substitution causes division by zero. Thus, it is discontinuous at $a = 2$.
> - **Type:** **Removable**. Simplifying gives $\frac{(x-2)(x+1)}{x-2} = x + 1$ for $x \neq 2$. The limit as $x \to 2$ is $3$.
> 
> **(b) $f(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{x^2} & \text{if } x \neq 0 \\ 1 & \text{if } x = 0 \end{cases}$**
> - **Analysis:** Here, $f(0) = 1$ is defined. However, $\lim_{x \to 0} \frac{1}{x^2} = \infty$, so the limit does not exist.
> - **Type:** **Infinite** discontinuity at $a = 0$.
> 
> **(c) $f(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{x^2 - x - 2}{x - 2} & \text{if } x \neq 2 \\ 1 & \text{if } x = 2 \end{cases}$**
> - **Analysis:** Here, $f(2) = 1$. The limit as $x \to 2$ is $3$ (from part a). Because $\lim_{x \to 2} f(x) \neq f(2)$, it is discontinuous at $a = 2$.
> - **Type:** **Removable** (the hole exists but the dot is placed at a different height).
> 
> **(d) $f(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$ (The Greatest Integer Function)**
> - **Analysis:** This function drops down to the nearest lower integer at every whole number step. As seen in Section 1.5, the left-hand and right-hand limits are never equal at integer thresholds.
> - **Type:** **Jump** discontinuities at all integers $\mathbb{Z}$.

## One-Sided Continuity & Interval Extensions

To handle endpoints of closed domain intervals cleanly, we define one-sided continuity.

> [!info] Definitions: Directional Continuity
> * A function $f$ is **continuous from the right** at a number $a$ if:
>   $$\lim_{x \to a^+} f(x) = f(a)$$
> * A function $f$ is **continuous from the left** at a number $a$ if:
>   $$\lim_{x \to a^-} f(x) = f(a)$$

### Continuity on an Interval
A function $f$ is **continuous on an interval** if it is continuous at every number inside that interval. At a closed endpoint, continuity is interpreted simply as directional continuity (left-continuous at the right endpoint, right-continuous at the left endpoint).

---

> [!example] Example 4: Continuity of a Semicircle over a Closed Window
> Show that the function $f(x) = 1 - \sqrt{1 - x^2}$ is continuous on the closed interval $[-1, 1]$.
> 
> **Step-by-Step Solution:**
> 1. **Check interior points ($-1 < a < 1$):** Using the Limit Laws:
>    $$\lim_{x \to a} \left(1 - \sqrt{1 - x^2}\right) = 1 - \sqrt{\lim_{x \to a}(1 - x^2)} = 1 - \sqrt{1 - a^2} = f(a)$$
>    Thus, $f$ is continuous at any open point inside $(-1, 1)$.
> 2. **Check the left endpoint ($a = -1$):** Evaluate the limit from the right side:
>    $$\lim_{x \to -1^+} f(x) = 1 - \sqrt{1 - (-1)^2} = 1 = f(-1)$$
>    So $f$ is continuous from the right at $-1$.
> 3. **Check the right endpoint ($a = 1$):** Evaluate the limit from the left side:
>    $$\lim_{x \to 1^-} f(x) = 1 - \sqrt{1 - (1)^2} = 1 = f(1)$$
>    So $f$ is continuous from the left at $1$.
> 
> **Conclusion:** By definition, $f$ is continuous on the entire closed interval $[-1, 1]$. Its graph forms the lower half of the circle $x^2 + (y - 1)^2 = 1$.

## Properties and Combinations of Continuous Functions

Continuity is preserved under standard arithmetic combinations.

> [!info] Theorem: Combinations
> If $f$ and $g$ are continuous at $a$ and $c$ is a constant, then the following functions are also continuous at $a$:
> 1. $f + g$
> 2. $f - g$
> 3. $c f$
> 4. $f g$
> 5. $\frac{f}{g} \quad (\text{provided } g(a) \neq 0)$

As a direct consequence, if $f$ and $g$ are continuous on an interval, their combinations are continuous across that same interval domain space.

---

### Domain Categories of Continuity

**functions are continuous everywhere within their natural algebraic domains.**

> [!important] Theorem: Continuous Functions Theorem
> The following types of functions are continuous at every single number inside their respective domains:
> * **Polynomials** (Continuous everywhere: $\mathbb{R}$)
> * **Rational Functions** (Continuous except where the denominator evaluates to zero)
> * **Root Functions** (Continuous across all non-negative elements for even indices)
> * **Trigonometric Functions** (e.g., $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ are continuous everywhere; $\tan x$ is continuous except at its vertical asymptotes)


---

> [!example] Example 5: Instantaneous Evaluation of a Rational Limit
> Find $\lim_{x \to -2} \frac{x^3 + 2x^2 - 1}{5 - 3x}$.
> 
> **Solution:**
> The function $f(x) = \frac{x^3 + 2x^2 - 1}{5 - 3x}$ is a rational function. Its domain excludes only values where $5 - 3x = 0 \implies x = \frac{5}{3}$. 
> Since $a = -2$ is securely within its domain, the function is continuous at $-2$. We can evaluate the limit instantly using direct substitution:
> $$\lim_{x \to -2} \frac{x^3 + 2x^2 - 1}{5 - 3x} = f(-2) = \frac{(-2)^3 + 2(-2)^2 - 1}{5 - 3(-2)} = -\frac{1}{11}$$

#### Continuity Analysis of Complex Expressions

> [!example] Example 6: Finding Intervals of Continuity
> Determine the intervals of continuity for each given function mapping:
> 
> **(a) $f(x) = x^{100} - 2x^{37} + 75$**
> - **Analysis:** $f(x)$ is a polynomial function. Polynomials are structurally continuous everywhere across the real line.
> - **Interval:** $(-\infty, \infty)$
> 
> **(b) $g(x) = \frac{x^2 + 2x + 17}{x^2 - 1}$**
> - **Analysis:** $g(x)$ is a rational function, which means it is continuous everywhere except where its denominator is equal to zero.
>   $$x^2 - 1 = 0 \implies x^2 = 1 \implies x = \pm 1$$
> - **Intervals:** $(-\infty, -1) \cup (-1, 1) \cup (1, \infty)$
> 
> **(c) $h(x) = \sqrt{x} + \frac{x + 1}{x - 1} - \frac{x + 1}{x^2 + 1}$**
> Break this combination down into separate components: $h(x) = F(x) + G(x) - H(x)$
> - $F(x) = \sqrt{x} \implies$ Continuous on its natural domain $[0, \infty)$.
> - $G(x) = \frac{x + 1}{x - 1} \implies$ Rational function; discontinuous at $x - 1 = 0 \implies x = 1$.
> - $H(x) = \frac{x + 1}{x^2 + 1} \implies$ Rational function; denominator $x^2 + 1 \ge 1$ is never zero, so it is continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$.
> - **Intersection of Domains:** Combining the conditions ($x \ge 0$ and $x \neq 1$) yields the final continuity intervals.
> - **Intervals:** $[0, 1) \cup (1, \infty)$

---

> [!example] Example 7: Continuous Limit Evaluation via Identities
> Evaluate the trigonometric limit: $\lim_{x \to \pi} \frac{\sin x}{2 + \cos x}$.
> 
> **Step-by-Step Analysis:**
> 1. The numerator $y = \sin x$ is continuous everywhere.
> 2. The denominator $y = 2 + \cos x$ is the sum of two continuous functions, making it continuous. Since $\cos x \ge -1$, the denominator satisfies $2 + \cos x \ge 1 > 0$ and is never zero.
> 3. Because the quotient is a combination of continuous operations with a non-zero denominator, $f(x) = \frac{\sin x}{2 + \cos x}$ is continuous everywhere.
> 4. Apply the Direct Substitution Property:
>    $$\lim_{x \to \pi} \frac{\sin x}{2 + \cos x} = f(\pi) = \frac{\sin \pi}{2 + \cos \pi} = \frac{0}{2 - 1} = 0$$

## Continuity of Composite Functions

Pairs of continuous functions can be combined via composition to build new continuous tracking networks.

> [!info] Theorem 8: Limit of a Composite Function
> If $f$ is continuous at $b$ and $\lim_{x \to a} g(x) = b$, then:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(g(x)) = f(b)$$
> In other words, the limit operation can be moved inside the function argument:
> $$\lim_{x \to a} f(g(x)) = f\left(\lim_{x \to a} g(x)\right)$$

*Application to Roots:* When $f(x) = \sqrt[n]{x}$, this theorem justifies Limit Law 11: $\lim_{x \to a} \sqrt[n]{g(x)} = \sqrt[n]{\lim_{x \to a} g(x)}$.

---

> [!info] Theorem 9: Continuity of Composites
> If $g$ is continuous at $a$ and $f$ is continuous at $g(a)$, then the composite function $f \circ g$ given by $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$ is continuous at $a$.
> 
> *Informal Summary:* **"A continuous function of a continuous function is a continuous function."**

---

> [!example] Example 8: Identifying Composite Continuity Intervals
> Determine where each composite function is continuous:
> 
> **(a) $h(x) = \sin(x^2)$**
> - **Analysis:** Let $g(x) = x^2$ (an everywhere-continuous polynomial) and $f(x) = \sin x$ (an everywhere-continuous trigonometric wave). 
> - **Result:** By Theorem 9, the composite function $h(x) = f(g(x))$ is continuous everywhere on $\mathbb{R}$.
> 
> **(b) $F(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2 + 7} - 4}$**
> - **Decomposition Analysis:** $F(x)$ can be broken down as a four-layer deep chain composition $f \circ g \circ h \circ k$, or $f(g(h(k(x))))$, where:
>   $$f(x) = \frac{1}{x}, \quad g(x) = x - 4, \quad h(x) = \sqrt{x}, \quad k(x) = x^2 + 7$$
> - **Finding Exclusions:** Each individual component is continuous across its natural domain. By Theorem 9, $F(x)$ is continuous everywhere except where its denominator is zero:
>   $$\sqrt{x^2 + 7} - 4 = 0 \implies \sqrt{x^2 + 7} = 4$$
>   Squaring both sides:
>   $$x^2 + 7 = 16 \implies x^2 = 9 \implies x = \pm 3$$
> - **Intervals:** $(-\infty, -3) \cup (-3, 3) \cup (3, \infty)$

## The Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)

> [!info] Theorem Formulation
> Suppose that $f$ is continuous on the closed interval $[a, b]$ and let $N$ be any number between $f(a)$ and $f(b)$, where $f(a) \neq f(b)$. Then there exists a number $c$ in the open interval $(a, b)$ such that:
> $$f(c) = N$$

### Geometric Interpretation
The Intermediate Value Theorem states that a continuous function takes on every intermediate value between its starting height $f(a)$ and ending height $f(b)$. 
- Geometrically, if you draw any horizontal line $y = N$ between the boundary heights $y = f(a)$ and $y = f(b)$, the continuous curve $y = f(x)$ cannot jump over it. The graph **must intersect** the line $y = N$ at least once.
- **Multiplicity:** The value $N$ can be taken on exactly once, or multiple times (e.g., at distinct values $c_1, c_2, c_3$).

![[Pasted image 20260626024634.png]]

> [!warning] Essential Condition
> The function $f$ **must be continuous**. The theorem does not hold for discontinuous functions, which can jump directly over the horizontal line $y = N$ without crossing it.

---

### Root Location Application
A primary application of the IVT is proving the existence of roots (zeros) of equations. If a continuous function changes signs over an interval (from negative to positive, or vice versa), taking $N = 0$ guarantees that the curve must cross the $x$-axis.

---

> [!example] Example 9: Proving the Existence of a Root
> Show that there is a root of the equation $4x^3 - 6x^2 + 3x - 2 = 0$ between $1$ and $2$.
> 
> **Step-by-Step Solution:**
> 1. **Define the function and verify continuity:**
>    Let $f(x) = 4x^3 - 6x^2 + 3x - 2$. Because $f$ is a polynomial, it is continuous everywhere, including on the closed interval $[1, 2]$.
> 2. **Evaluate the endpoints:**
>    - At $x = 1$: $f(1) = 4(1)^3 - 6(1)^2 + 3(1) - 2 = 4 - 6 + 3 - 2 = -1 \quad (< 0)$
>    - At $x = 2$: $f(2) = 4(2)^3 - 6(2)^2 + 3(2) - 2 = 32 - 24 + 6 - 2 = 12 \quad (> 0)$
> 3. **Apply the IVT:**
>    Since $f(1) = -1$ and $f(2) = 12$, the value $N = 0$ lies securely between them ($f(1) < 0 < f(2)$). 
>    Therefore, by the Intermediate Value Theorem, there must exist a number $c$ in the open interval $(1, 2)$ such that $f(c) = 0$. 
>    
>    **Conclusion:** The equation has at least one real root in the interval $(1, 2)$.

---

### Numerical Refinement (Bisection Method Concept)
We can locate the root more precisely by repeatedly narrowing down the interval where the sign change occurs:

* **Iteration 1:** Check midpoints between $1$ and $2$:
  - $f(1.2) = -0.128 < 0$
  - $f(1.3) = 0.548 > 0$
  $\implies$ A sign change occurs between $1.2$ and $1.3$, so the root lies in $(1.2, 1.3)$.

* **Iteration 2:** Check smaller sub-intervals:
  - $f(1.22) = -0.007008 < 0$
  - $f(1.23) = 0.056068 > 0$
  $\implies$ The root lies more precisely within the interval $(1.22, 1.23)$.
