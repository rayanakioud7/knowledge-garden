## Vectors and Core Operations

The heart of linear algebra lies in two fundamental operations: adding vectors and multiplying vectors by numbers (scalars). These operations build the foundation for moving into $n$-dimensional space.

> [!info] Definition: Column Vector
> A vector $v$ is standardly written as a column, not a row, to keep its components distinct.
> 
> 
> $$v = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> 
> Here, $v_1$ is the first component (movement along the $x$-axis) and $v_2$ is the second component (movement along the $y$-axis). To save space in text, a column vector is sometimes written in row form with parentheses and commas: $v = (v_1, v_2)$.
> 
> 

> [!warning] Pitfall: Row vs. Column Vectors
> The row form $v = (1, 1, -1)$ is just a space-saving typographic convention. It is in actuality a column vector temporarily lying down. A true $1 \times 3$ row vector like $\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & -1 \end{bmatrix}$ is geometrically the same but mathematically represents the "transpose" of the column vector.
> 
> 

### Vector Addition and Scalar Multiplication

Vector addition is performed component by component.

$$v + w = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} + \begin{bmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 + w_1 \\ v_2 + w_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

Scalar multiplication involves multiplying each component of a vector by a constant number $c$.

$$cv = c \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} cv_1 \\ cv_2 \end{bmatrix}$$

## Linear Combinations

> [!abstract] Core Principle: Linear Combinations
> Combining addition and scalar multiplication produces a **linear combination** of the vectors. By multiplying vector $v$ by scalar $c$, vector $w$ by scalar $d$, and adding the results, you obtain the linear combination $cv + dw$.
> 
> 
> $$cv + dw = c \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ v_2 \end{bmatrix} + d \begin{bmatrix} w_1 \\ w_2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} cv_1 + dw_1 \\ cv_2 + dw_2 \end{bmatrix}$$
> 
> 

Special cases of linear combinations include the sum ($1v + 1w$), the difference ($1v - 1w$), and the zero vector ($0v + 0w$). The zero vector is always a possible linear combination of any set of vectors.

## The Geometry of Combinations (The Big Picture)

Understanding the geometry of linear combinations is the key to linear algebra. If you take *all* possible combinations of a set of vectors by allowing the scalars to be any real numbers, the resulting combinations will geometrically "fill" spaces of varying dimensions.

| Vector Combinations             | Geometric Picture in 3D Space ($u, v, w \in \mathbb{R}^3$)                       |
| ------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| All multiples $cu$              | Fills a **line** passing through the origin $(0,0,0)$.                           |
| All combinations $cu + dv$      | Fills a **plane** passing through the origin $(0,0,0)$.                          |
| All combinations $cu + dv + ew$ | Fills the entire **three-dimensional space** (if $w$ is not on the $u,v$ plane). |

## Worked Examples

### Example 1: Describing a Plane in $\mathbb{R}^3$

Describe the plane generated by the combinations of $v = (1, 1, 0)$ and $w = (0, 1, 1)$. Find a vector that is *not* a combination of $v$ and $w$.

**Step-by-Step Derivation:**
The plane contains all combinations $cv + dw$.

$$cv + dw = c \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} + d \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c \\ c+d \\ d \end{bmatrix}$$

For any point in this plane, the second component ($c+d$) must always exactly equal the sum of the first component ($c$) and the third component ($d$).

Therefore, a vector like $\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 3 \end{bmatrix}$ is **not** on the plane because the components do not satisfy the required relationship: $2 \neq 1 + 3$.

### Example 2: Solving for Linear Combination Coefficients

Find two equations for scalars $c$ and $d$ so that the linear combination $cv + dw$ equals a target vector $b$:

$$v = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix}, \quad w = \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}, \quad b = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

**Step-by-Step Derivation:**
Set up the vector equation:

$$c \begin{bmatrix} 2 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix} + d \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

Split the vector equation into two ordinary scalar equations by looking at the components separately:

$$\begin{aligned}
2c - d &= 1 \\
-c + 2d &= 0
\end{aligned}$$

Solving this system (each equation represents a line, and their intersection is the solution) yields $c = \frac{2}{3}$ and $d = \frac{1}{3}$.

This fundamental setup can also be written natively as a **matrix equation**, which is the primary direction of the subject:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & -1 \\ -1 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} c \\ d \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}$$
