
# Levels of Causation: The Architecture of Events

> [!abstract] The Core Concept
> Historians rarely accept a single reason for why an event occurred. Answering "Why did this happen?" requires building a hierarchy of factors. Not all causes are equal, and ranking them is how historians uncover the true mechanics of an era.

## 1. The Anatomy of a Cause

Causation involves exploring both the immediate sparks and the deep, underlying structures that make an event possible. 

| Level | Definition | The Role it Plays |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Primary** | The immediate "spark" or trigger. | The direct action that ignites the event. Without the underlying conditions, it might have just been a minor incident. |
| **Secondary** | The intermediate context. | Ongoing policies, mid-term decisions, or shifting alliances that set the stage and make the primary spark dangerous. |
| **Tertiary** | The structural background. | The deep-rooted geopolitical, economic, or cultural forces that build the tension over decades or centuries. |

---

## 2. Historical Applications

Ranking these causes is where the actual work of a historian happens. By arguing which level was the *most* responsible, we shape our interpretation of the past.

### Case A: The U.S. Enters WWII (1941)

* **Primary:** The Japanese bombing of Pearl Harbor (December 7, 1941). 
* **Secondary:** President Roosevelt’s pre-war policies, such as the Lend-Lease Act, which economically supported Britain and alienated Axis powers.
* **Tertiary:** The decades-old geopolitical rivalry between the US and Japan for economic dominance over the Pacific Rim.

> [!note] The Analytical Debate
> The immediate shock of Pearl Harbor is the undeniable primary cause. However, a structural historian would argue that the tertiary rivalry made a conflict inevitable; Pearl Harbor merely determined *when* and *how* it started.

### Case B: The Siege of Constantinople (1453)

* **Primary:** [[Ottoman Empire|Sultan Mehmed II]]’s intense personal ambition to prove his legitimacy and solidify his rule.
* **Secondary:** [[Roman Empire|Constantinople]]'s geographic position as the ultimate chokepoint between Eastern and Western trade routes.
* **Tertiary:** The long-standing Ottoman tradition of military expansion, which inherently required constant conquest to sustain its economy and political morale.

> [!note] The Analytical Debate
> Did Constantinople fall primarily because Mehmed was uniquely driven (Agency), or because the Ottoman war machine structurally required expansion to survive (Determinism)?

## 3. The Historian's Framework

When analyzing any event, keep these four principles in mind:

1. **Causation is a Pyramid:** The broad, deep base (tertiary) supports the middle (secondary), which culminates in the tip (primary).
2. **Justify the Rank:** Simply listing causes isn't enough. The historical method requires defending *why* one factor outweighs another.
3. **Agency vs. Structure:** Notice how Primary causes often involve human decisions (Agency), while Tertiary causes involve broad societal shifts (Structure).
4. **Embrace Disagreement:** The facts of an event are usually undisputed; the *weight* assigned to the causes is where historians debate.

## 4. Analytical Exercise

To test this framework:
1. Select an event you know well (e.g., a technological breakthrough, a major conflict, or even a shift in local politics).
2. Identify at least one Primary, Secondary, and Tertiary cause.
3. **The Challenge:** Write a brief argument defending why the *Tertiary* cause was actually more critical than the *Primary* trigger.
