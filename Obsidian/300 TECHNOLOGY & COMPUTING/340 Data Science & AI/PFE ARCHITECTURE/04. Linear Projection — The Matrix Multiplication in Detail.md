> **One sentence summary:** The linear projection multiplies the
> input matrix $(64, 690)$ by the weight matrix $(690, 256)$ to
> produce the output matrix $(64, 256)$ — the same 64 frames,
> but each frame is now described by 256 learned features instead
> of 690 raw coordinates.

---

## 1. The Three Matrices

Before doing any multiplication, let us understand exactly what
each matrix contains.

### The Input Matrix $\mathbf{X}$

Shape: $(64, 345)$

```text
          coord₁  coord₂  coord₃  ···  coord₃₄₅
          (x_lm0) (y_lm0) (z_lm0)     (z_lm114)

frame 1  [ 0.12   -0.45    0.03  ···   0.71  ]
frame 2  [ 0.15   -0.43    0.04  ···   0.68  ]
frame 3  [ 0.18   -0.40    0.06  ···   0.65  ]
  ·
  ·
frame 64 [ 0.00    0.00    0.00  ···   0.00  ]  ← padding frame
```

- **64 rows** — one per time frame (the sign video)
- **345 columns** — the raw landmark coordinates
  (grouped: 115 x-coords, 115 y-coords, 115 z-coords)
- The last frames are zeros because the sign was shorter than
  64 frames and was center-padded

### The Weight Matrix $\mathbf{W}$

Shape: $(690, 256)$

```text
            p₁      p₂      p₃    ···    p₂₅₆
          (perc1) (perc2) (perc3)       (perc256)

coord₁   [ 0.23  -0.11    0.07  ···   -0.44 ]
coord₂   [-0.08   0.31   -0.19  ···    0.12 ]
coord₃   [ 0.15  -0.27    0.41  ···   -0.03 ]
  ·
  ·
coord_690 [-0.09   0.14    0.08  ···    0.37 ]
```

- **690 rows** — one weight per input coordinate
- **256 columns** — one column per perceptron
- **Each column** is one perceptron's complete weight vector
  of length 690

> Column $j$ of $\mathbf{W}$ contains all 690 weights that
> perceptron $j$ uses. It answers: "how much does each of the
> 345 coordinates matter for computing learned feature $j$?"

### The Output Matrix $\mathbf{H}^{(0)}$

Shape: $(64, 256)$

```text
          feat₁   feat₂   feat₃  ···   feat₂₅₆
        ("raised") ("open") ("twist")  ("point")

frame 1  [ 0.91    0.12   -0.33  ···    0.44 ]
frame 2  [ 0.88    0.14   -0.31  ···    0.41 ]
frame 3  [ 0.84    0.17   -0.28  ···    0.38 ]
  ·
  ·
frame 64 [ 0.00    0.00    0.00  ···    0.00 ]  ← still zeros
```

- **64 rows** — still one per frame (unchanged)
- **256 columns** — now learned features, not raw coordinates

## 2. The Rule of Matrix Multiplication

Before the full example, here is the rule with tiny matrices.

Given $\mathbf{A} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times k}$ and
$\mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{k \times n}$:

$$\mathbf{C} = \mathbf{A} \mathbf{B} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}$$

The **inner dimensions must match** ($k = k$).
The **outer dimensions survive** ($m$ and $n$).

For your case:

$$\underbrace{\mathbf{X}}_{(64,\ 690)}
\times
\underbrace{\mathbf{W}}_{(690,\ 256)}
=
\underbrace{\mathbf{H}^{(0)}}_{(64,\ 256)}$$

$$\underbrace{64}_{\text{frames}}
\times
\underbrace{\cancel{345} \times \cancel{345}}_{\text{cancel}}
\times
\underbrace{256}_{\text{features}}
\quad \Rightarrow \quad
(64,\ 256)$$

---

## 3. How Each Cell Is Computed

The output cell at row $t$ (frame $t$) and column $j$ (feature $j$)
is computed as:

$$H^{(0)}_{t,j} = \sum_{i=1}^{690} X_{t,i} \cdot W_{i,j} + b_j$$

In plain English:

> Take frame $t$'s 690 coordinates.
> Multiply each one by perceptron $j$'s corresponding weight.
> Add them all up.
> Add perceptron $j$'s bias.
> That gives you feature $j$ of frame $t$.

This is the dot product between:
- **Row $t$** of $\mathbf{X}$ — the 690 coordinates of frame $t$
- **Column $j$** of $\mathbf{W}$ — perceptron $j$'s 690 weights

$$H^{(0)}_{t,j} = \mathbf{x}_t \cdot \mathbf{w}_j + b_j$$

## 4. A Tiny Worked Example

Let us do this with real numbers on a miniature version:
2 frames, 3 input coordinates, 2 perceptrons.

$$\mathbf{X} =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.8 & -0.3 & 0.5 \\
0.2 & 0.6 & -0.1
\end{bmatrix}
\quad (2 \times 3)$$

$$\mathbf{W} =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.4 & -0.2 \\
0.1 & 0.7 \\
-0.3 & 0.5
\end{bmatrix}
\quad (3 \times 2)$$

$$\mathbf{b} = \begin{bmatrix} 0.1 \\ -0.1 \end{bmatrix}$$

### Computing $H_{1,1}$ — Frame 1, Feature 1 (Perceptron 1)

$$H_{1,1} = (0.8)(0.4) + (-0.3)(0.1) + (0.5)(-0.3) + 0.1$$
$$= 0.32 - 0.03 - 0.15 + 0.1 = \mathbf{0.24}$$

### Computing $H_{1,2}$ — Frame 1, Feature 2 (Perceptron 2)

$$H_{1,2} = (0.8)(-0.2) + (-0.3)(0.7) + (0.5)(0.5) + (-0.1)$$
$$= -0.16 - 0.21 + 0.25 - 0.1 = \mathbf{-0.22}$$

### Computing $H_{2,1}$ — Frame 2, Feature 1 (Perceptron 1)

$$H_{2,1} = (0.2)(0.4) + (0.6)(0.1) + (-0.1)(-0.3) + 0.1$$
$$= 0.08 + 0.06 + 0.03 + 0.1 = \mathbf{0.27}$$

### Computing $H_{2,2}$ — Frame 2, Feature 2 (Perceptron 2)

$$H_{2,2} = (0.2)(-0.2) + (0.6)(0.7) + (-0.1)(0.5) + (-0.1)$$
$$= -0.04 + 0.42 - 0.05 - 0.1 = \mathbf{0.23}$$

### Final output:

$$\mathbf{H}^{(0)} =
\begin{bmatrix}
0.24 & -0.22 \\
0.27 & 0.23
\end{bmatrix}
\quad (2 \times 2)$$

## 5. Back to Your Real Dimensions

The exact same process happens in your model, just at scale:

```text
For every frame t in {1, 2, ..., 64}:
    For every perceptron j in {1, 2, ..., 256}:

        H[t, j] = X[t, 0] × W[0, j]
                + X[t, 1] × W[1, j]
                + X[t, 2] × W[2, j]
                + ...
                + X[t, 689] × W[689, j]
                + b[j]

That is 345 multiplications + 689 additions + 1 bias = 690 operations
per cell. There are 64 × 256 = 16,384 cells.

Total operations: 16,384 × 345 ≈ 5.65 million multiplications
per sample per forward pass.
```

A GPU does all 5.65 million of these **simultaneously** using
its thousands of cores. On a CPU this would take milliseconds.
On your RX 7900 XT with 5,376 stream processors this takes
**microseconds** — which is why one forward pass costs only ~5 ms.

## 6. What Each of the 256 Features Actually Represents

After training, each column of $\mathbf{W}$ (each perceptron)
has specialised to detect a particular pattern in the 690
landmark coordinates.

We cannot read off what each feature means directly — the weights
are 345 numbers that we cannot easily interpret. But we can infer
from what the model learns to classify:

```text
Feature j=1  might have learned:
  W[51, 1] = +2.1   ← left shoulder x (high weight = very important)
  W[52, 1] = -1.8   ← right shoulder x (negative = opposite direction)
  W[73, 1] = +0.9   ← left wrist x
  ...
  → This perceptron fires when the left arm is raised
    relative to the right shoulder

Feature j=2  might have learned:
  W[0, 2]  = +1.4   ← upper lip center (index 0)
  W[14, 2] = -1.2   ← inner lower lip (index 14)
  → This perceptron fires when the mouth is open
    (upper and lower lip moving apart)
```

Nobody programmed these interpretations. The model discovered
them automatically because they help distinguish between the
250 ASL signs during training.

## 7. Why Not Skip This Step?

You might ask: why not feed the 690 raw coordinates directly
into the Transformer?

Technically you could. But two problems arise:

**Problem 1 — Unit inconsistency**

The 345 features are not on the same scale:
- x-coordinates: typically in range $[-2, +2]$ shoulder-widths
- z-coordinates (depth): range can reach $[-5, +5]$, noisier

The Transformer's attention mechanism would be dominated by
whichever coordinates have the largest numerical values —
not necessarily the most linguistically important ones.

The projection learns to **rescale** everything into a
consistent 256-dimensional space.

**Problem 2 — Inefficiency**

The Transformer's attention mechanism computes similarities
between every pair of frames. With 64 frames and 345 features:

$$\text{Attention cost} \propto 64^2 \times 345 = 1{,}433{,}600$$

With 64 frames and 256 features (after projection):

$$\text{Attention cost} \propto 64^2 \times 256 = 1{,}048{,}576$$

The projection reduces attention computation by 27%.
More importantly, reducing from 345 to 256 makes the
attention heads' dimension exactly $256/8 = 32$ — a clean
and efficient split.

## 8. The Complete Shape Transformation

$$\underbrace{(B,\ T,\ 690)}_{\text{raw landmarks}}
\xrightarrow{\mathbf{W}\ (690 \times 256)\ +\ \mathbf{b}}
\underbrace{(B,\ T,\ 256)}_{\text{learned features}}$$

Where:
- $B$ = batch size (32 or 64 samples processed simultaneously)
- $T$ = temporal length (64 frames)
- 345 = raw features (landmark coordinates)
- 256 = $d_{\text{model}}$ (learned features)

The batch dimension $B$ and time dimension $T$ are both
**preserved exactly**. The linear projection only operates
on the last dimension — replacing 345 with 256.

## 9. Summary

| Question                                      | Answer                                                      |
| --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| What goes in?                                 | $(64, 690)$ — 64 frames, each with 345 coordinates          |
| What comes out?                               | $(64, 256)$ — 64 frames, each with 256 learned features     |
| What is $\mathbf{W}$?                         | $(345, 256)$ — 256 columns, each a perceptron's 345 weights |
| How many parameters?                          | $690 \times 256 + 256 = 88,576$                             |
| Is the same $\mathbf{W}$ used for all frames? | **Yes** — shared across all 64 frames and all 32 samples    |
| Does $\mathbf{W}$ change during inference?    | **No** — frozen after training                              |
| What does each output feature represent?      | A learned combination of all 345 input coordinates          |

## 10. The Equation, Fully Annotated

$$\underbrace{\mathbf{H}^{(0)}}_{(64,256)} =
\underbrace{\mathbf{X}}_{(64,690)}
\underbrace{\mathbf{W}_{\text{proj}}}_{(690,256)} +
\underbrace{\mathbf{b}}_{(256,)}$$

The bias vector $\mathbf{b}$ of shape $(256,)$ is **broadcast**
across all 64 rows — the same bias is added to every frame's
output.

## 11. The Matrix Multiplication — Written Out Explicitly

---

### Convention 1 — Row-Vector (PyTorch Style)

**One frame at a time** ($1 \times 690$) times ($690 \times 256$):

$$
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1 & x_2 & x_3 & \cdots & x_{690}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{1 \times 690}
\times
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
w_{1,1}   & w_{1,2}   & \cdots & w_{1,256}   \\
w_{2,1}   & w_{2,2}   & \cdots & w_{2,256}   \\
w_{3,1}   & w_{3,2}   & \cdots & w_{3,256}   \\
\vdots    & \vdots    & \ddots & \vdots      \\
w_{690,1} & w_{690,2} & \cdots & w_{690,256}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{690 \times 256}
+
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
b_1 & b_2 & \cdots & b_{256}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{1 \times 256}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
h_1 & h_2 & \cdots & h_{256}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{1 \times 256}
$$

**All 64 frames at once** ($64 \times 345$) times ($345 \times 256$):

$$
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
x^{(1)}_1   & x^{(1)}_2   & \cdots & x^{(1)}_{690}   \\
x^{(2)}_1   & x^{(2)}_2   & \cdots & x^{(2)}_{690}   \\
x^{(3)}_1   & x^{(3)}_2   & \cdots & x^{(3)}_{690}   \\
\vdots      & \vdots      & \ddots & \vdots          \\
x^{(64)}_1  & x^{(64)}_2  & \cdots & x^{(64)}_{690}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\mathbf{X}\ (64 \times 690)}
\times
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
w_{1,1}   & w_{1,2}   & \cdots & w_{1,256}   \\
w_{2,1}   & w_{2,2}   & \cdots & w_{2,256}   \\
\vdots    & \vdots    & \ddots & \vdots      \\
w_{690,1} & w_{690,2} & \cdots & w_{690,256}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\mathbf{W}\ (690 \times 256)}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
h^{(1)}_1   & h^{(1)}_2   & \cdots & h^{(1)}_{256}   \\
h^{(2)}_1   & h^{(2)}_2   & \cdots & h^{(2)}_{256}   \\
h^{(3)}_1   & h^{(3)}_2   & \cdots & h^{(3)}_{256}   \\
\vdots      & \vdots      & \ddots & \vdots          \\
h^{(64)}_1  & h^{(64)}_2  & \cdots & h^{(64)}_{256}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\mathbf{H}^{(0)}\ (64 \times 256)}
$$

Where each cell $h^{(t)}_j$ is computed as:

$$h^{(t)}_j
= \sum_{i=1}^{690} x^{(t)}_i \cdot w_{i,j} + b_j
= x^{(t)}_1 w_{1,j}
+ x^{(t)}_2 w_{2,j}
+ \cdots
+ x^{(t)}_{690} w_{690,j}
+ b_j$$

- $t \in \{1, \ldots, 64\}$ — which frame
- $j \in \{1, \ldots, 256\}$ — which perceptron
- $i \in \{1, \ldots, 345\}$ — which input coordinate

---

### Convention 2 — Column-Vector (Academic Papers)

**One frame at a time** ($256 \times 345$) times ($345 \times 1$):

$$
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
w_{1,1}   & w_{1,2}   & \cdots & w_{1,345}   \\
w_{2,1}   & w_{2,2}   & \cdots & w_{2,345}   \\
w_{3,1}   & w_{3,2}   & \cdots & w_{3,345}   \\
\vdots    & \vdots    & \ddots & \vdots      \\
w_{256,1} & w_{256,2} & \cdots & w_{256,345}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\mathbf{W}^\top\ (256 \times 345)}
\times
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
x_1    \\
x_2    \\
x_3    \\
\vdots \\
x_{345}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\mathbf{x}\ (345 \times 1)}
+
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
b_1    \\
b_2    \\
\vdots \\
b_{256}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\mathbf{b}\ (256 \times 1)}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
h_1    \\
h_2    \\
\vdots \\
h_{256}
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\mathbf{h}^{(0)}\ (256 \times 1)}
$$

### The Cancellation Rule — Visualised

$$
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\cdot & \cdot & \cdots & \cdot
\end{bmatrix}
}_{1 \times \mathbf{345}}
\times
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\cdot & \cdots & \cdot \\
\cdot & \cdots & \cdot \\
\vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
\cdot & \cdots & \cdot
\end{bmatrix}
}_{\mathbf{345} \times 256}
=
\underbrace{
\begin{bmatrix}
\cdot & \cdot & \cdots & \cdot
\end{bmatrix}
}_{1 \times 256}
$$

$$\boxed{(m \times \cancel{k}) \times (\cancel{k} \times n) = (m \times n)}$$

The **345 cancels**. The frame count and feature count survive.
