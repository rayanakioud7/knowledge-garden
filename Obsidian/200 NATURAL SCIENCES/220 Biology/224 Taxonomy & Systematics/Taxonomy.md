## Introduction

> [!ABSTRACT] 
> The Dual Definition **1. Broad Sense:** The general practice and science of categorization—applicable to species, documents, videos, or concepts. **2. Strict Sense:** The specific biological methodology for classifying living and extinct organisms based on shared characteristics.

### The Two Pillars of Taxonomy

Taxonomy is more than just naming things; it is a two-step architectural process:

1. **The Scheme (Structure):** Developing the underlying system of classes (creating the "buckets").
2. **The Allocation (Classification):** The actual assignment of specific things into those classes (putting items into the "buckets").

> [!NOTE] 
> Evolution of the Term Originally, taxonomy referred exclusively to biology. Today, it has evolved into a universal principle used to organize **information systems**, **digital media**, and **abstract concepts**.

---

### Biological Taxonomy

> [!INFO] Etymology & Definition
> 
> **Taxonomy** derives from the Greek **$taxis$** ("arrangement") and **$nomos$** ("law").
> 
> Strictly speaking, it is the _methodology_ and _principles_ used to arrange living and extinct organisms into hierarchies of superior and subordinate groups.

### The Necessity of Formal Classification

Why do we need a scientific system? Popular ([^2]vernacular) classification is often superficial and confusing. It groups things by _utility_ or _habitat_ rather than biological reality.

> [!WARNING] The Vernacular Trap
> 
> **1. The "Fish" Fallacy:**
> 
> Historically, "fish" referred to anything aquatic (e.g., Shellfish, Starfish, Crayfish).
> 
> - **Reality:** A starfish shares fewer anatomical similarities with a bony fish than a bony fish shares with a human.
>     ![[Pasted image 20260203011053.png|center|300]]
> 
> **2. The "Robin" Ambiguity:**
> 
> Common names vary by region.
> 
> - **American Robin:** Turdus migratorius
>     
> - **English Robin:** Erithacus rubecula
>     
> - **Result:** The two birds are entirely different species, linked only by a name.
> 
>     ![[Pasted image 20260203011121.png|center|300]]

**The Linnaean Solution**
To solve this chaos, biologists rely on **~={cyan}[^3]Binomial Nomenclature=~**, the system established by Swedish naturalist **[[#4. The Linnaean system|Carolus Linnaeus]]** in the 1750s. This ensures every organism has a unique, internationally accepted name.

### Distinctions: Taxonomy vs. Systematics
While often used interchangeably, evolutionary biologist **[[Ernst Mayr]]** provides a critical distinction between the two fields:

| **Field**           | **Scope**       | **Focus**                                                                                                                |
| ------------------- | --------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **[[Taxonomy]]**    | **The Toolkit** | The _theory and practice_ of classifying organisms.                                                                      |
| **[[Systematics]]** | **The Science** | The science of the _diversity_ of organisms. It integrates [[Evolution]], [[Ecology]], [[Genetics]], and [[Physiology]]. |


> [!QUOTE] Ernst Mayr
> ![[Pasted image 20260203023534.png|center|500]] 
> "Taxonomy is the theory and practice of classifying organisms... [Systematics] is the science of the diversity of organisms."

## Historical Background

### 1. Early & Ancient Systems (Utility-Based)

Early classification was driven by **survival and utility** rather than scientific theory.

- **Indigenous Knowledge:** Local and practical. Recognizes major groups (fish, birds) but focuses on immediate needs (food, danger) rather than generalization.
![[Pasted image 20260203015106.png|center]]

- **Ancient China:** The _Shennong Ben Cao Jing_ (attributed to the mythical emperor [[Shennong]] ~2700 BCE, likely written ~1st millennium CE) cataloged **365 medicinal plants**.
![[Pasted image 20260203015015.png|center|400]]

- **Ancient Egypt:** Medical [^1]papyri (1700–1600 BCE) classified plants by their therapeutic uses for treating illness and injury.

![[Pasted image 20260203015334.png|center|300]]

### 2. The Aristotelian Era (The Logic of Essence)

**[[Aristotle]]** was the first great generalizer of Western classification. His method dominated science for nearly 2,000 years until the 19th century.

![[Pasted image 20260203020144.png|center|400]]

- **Methodology:** He applied the principles of **logic** and **geometry** to biology.
    - _Goal:_ To identify the **"Immutable Essence"** of an organism (what makes a thing _what it is_).
    - _Process:_ Discarded "accidental" (variable) characters to find "constant" characters via **induction**.
        
- **Key Insights:**
    - Recognized that [[Cetaceans]] (whales/dolphins) were mammals, not fish.
    - Separated invertebrates into distinct groups.
        
- **Limitations:** Lacked an evolutionary perspective and microscopy; viewed species as fixed/unchanging definitions.
    

> [!NOTE] 
> The 1,400-Year Gap After Aristotle and his student **[[Theophrastus]]**, biological classification stalled for over a millennium. It wasn't until the **Renaissance** (1543) that biology re-awakened with **[[Andreas Vesalius]]** (anatomy) and the founding of the first university botanic gardens (Padua, 1545).

### 3. The Pre-Linnaean Synthesis: John Ray

In the late 17th century, English naturalist **[[John Ray]]** bridged the gap between ancient logic and modern science.

![[Pasted image 20260203021746.png|center|400]]

- **Empiricism:** He tempered Aristotelian logic with strict **empirical observation**.
- **Major Contributions:**
    - Defined the **[[Species Concept]]** as the basic unit of classification.
    - Distinguished **[[Monocots]]** from **[[Dicots]]** (1703).
    - Correctly identified the affinities of whales (cementing Aristotle's earlier observation).

### 4. The Linnaean system

**Carolus Linnaeus** is considered the founder of modern [[Taxonomy]]. His work established the rules for identifying and naming living organisms, culminating in the consistent use of **binomial nomenclature** starting in **1758**.

![[Pasted image 20260203030305.png|center|500]]
#### Core Contributions
While Linnaeus attempted a natural classification system, his immediate success came from providing practical tools for scientists:
* **Hierarchical Structure:** He introduced the standard rank hierarchy:
    * Class
    * Order
    * [[Genus]]
    * [[Species]]
* **Identification Keys:** He created workable keys for identifying plants and animals. notably using the smaller parts of flowers to classify plants.
#### Classification Logic
Linnaeus's approach was fundamentally **Aristotelian**, based on the logic of "essential features."
* **Animals:** He divided animals into six groups. While the first four were accurate, the last two were overly broad compared to Aristotle's original groupings:
    1.  [[Mammals]]
    2.  [[Birds]]
    3.  [[Reptiles]]
    4.  [[Fishes]]
    5.  [[Insects]] (Lumped group)
    6.  [[Worms]] (Lumped group)
![[Pasted image 20260203030518.png|centern|550]]

#### The Evolution of Binomial Nomenclature
The shift to the modern two-name system was a practical solution to an overly complex problem.
##### 1. The Aristotelian Method (Old)
Previously, organisms were defined by **Genus + Differentia**:
* **Genus:** The general kind of thing (e.g., "Bird" or "Animal").
* **Differentia:** The special character distinguishing it (e.g., "Feeding in water").
* *Problem:* As more species were discovered, the *differentia* descriptions became unmanageably long.
##### 2. The Linnaean Solution (New)
Linnaeus began printing a "catch name" in the margins of his books. This consisted of the **Genus** + **One word** from the differentia (or a former name). This shorthand became the official scientific name.

> [!NOTE]
> **Examples:**
> * *[[Human Evolution|Homo sapiens]]* (Modern humans)
> * *Homo neanderthalensis* ([[Neanderthals]])
> * *Gorilla gorilla* (Gorilla)

### 5. Classification Since Linnaeus

Since the time of Linnaeus, taxonomy has evolved from a static grouping system to one that incorporates evolutionary history and new biological discoveries, striving for a "natural system" of classification.

#### 1. Major Refinements & Key Figures
As new biological data emerged, organisms were re-grouped based on internal anatomy and life history rather than superficial traits.

* **Jean-Baptiste Lamarck:**
	 ![[Pasted image 20260203032344.png|center|300]]
    * Separated **spiders** and **crustaceans** from insects.
    * Established the distinction between [[Vertebrates]] (with backbones) and [[Invertebrates]] (without backbones).
    * *Note:* Invertebrates constitute ~90% of animal diversity, though the group is defined solely by a lacking feature.
	    ![[Pasted image 20260203033147.png|center|400]]
* **Felix Dujardin:** Broke up the "Infusoria" (a catch-all group for microscopic aquatic life) into empirically recognized groups.
	![[Pasted image 20260203033242.png|center|300]]
* **Ernst Haeckel (1866):** Proposed the kingdom **[^4]Protista** to classify unicellular organisms, recognizing that the distinction between plants and animals blurs at the single-cell level.
	![[Pasted image 20260203033319.png|center|400]]
	- **Protista:**
		![[Pasted image 20260203033516.png|center|400]]
* **The Barnacle Reclassification:** Once their life history was understood, [[Barnacles]] were moved from [[Mollusks]] to [[Arthropods]] (specifically crustaceans).

#### 2. Timeline of Key Discoveries
Technological and paleontological advances filled in the gaps of the tree of life.

* **1851:** **Wilhelm Hofmeister** described the ~={pink}Alternation of Generations=~ in cryptogamous (non-flowering) plants, clarifying high-level plant divisions. 

![[Pasted image 20260203034837.png|center|400]]

**Alternation of Generations:**  means that *plants alternate between two different life stages, or generations, in their life cycle*; a haploid stage called gametophyte and a diploid stage called sporophyte. The terms haploid and diploid refer to the number of chromosomes contained in the cells
~={blue}For example=~, most of the cells in the human body contain two sets of chromosomes (n) meaning they are diploid (2n): one set that we inherit from our mother (n) and one set we inherit from our father (n). However, humans also have special reproductive cells (eggs and sperm) that only have one set of chromosomes, and we call this haploid (n). These reproductive cells are called gametes and they are produced via cell meiosis (reductive cell division). When a male gamete (n) joins a female gamete (n) through fertilization, they form a diploid organism (2n).

![[Pasted image 20260203035007.png]]

* **1859:** Discovery of Primitive spore-bearing land plants (**Psilophyta**) from the [[Cambrian Period]].
* **1898:** Discovery of viral agents (passing through filters).
* **1935:** First completely purified [[Virus]] obtained.
* **20th Century:** Recognition of the phylum Pogonophora (beardworms).

#### 3. The Impact of Darwinian Evolution
The acceptance of [[Charles Darwin|Darwinism]] shifted the focus from "natural affinity" (similarity of characters) to **evolutionary descent**.

![[Pasted image 20260203121430.png|center|550]]
##### Immediate Impact (The "Earthworm Confusion")
Initially, applying evolutionary theory caused confusion in groups without a fossil record. Taxonomists often arbitrarily decided which forms were "primitive" and which were "advanced."
* *Example:* The **Oligochaeta** ([[Earthworms]]).
    * Originally arranged from complex to simple.
    * Post-Darwin, scientists assumed the simple worm *Aeolosoma* was primitive and arranged the group from simple to complex.
    * Later, it was realized *Aeolosoma* might be **secondarily simplified** (evolved from complex ancestors), rendering the classification logic flawed.

##### Long-term Impact (Phylogenetics)
Where good [[Fossil Records]] existed, forms could be placed according to actual [^5]strata, allowing for the creation of valid **Phylogenetic Trees** ([[Classification Hiérarchique Ascendante (CAH)|dendrograms]]).

![[Pasted image 20260203121623.png]]

#### 4. Modern Challenges in Classification
Darwinian theory suggests life should be arranged in a continuous tree, not discrete classes. However, several factors make a perfect phylogenetic classification difficult:

* **Arbitrary Divisions:** Extensive fossil sequences must be broken up arbitrarily to create names.
* **Species Definition:** A simple definition of "species" is often impossible due to:
    * **Geographical Variation:** Wide variance within a species.
    * **Reticulate Evolution:** Plants often form species and then hybridize again.
    * **Asexual Reproduction:** The criterion of "interbreeding pools" cannot apply to organisms that do not breed sexually.
    * **Viruses:** Do not correspond to the species concepts used for higher organisms.

## Current Systems of Classification: Beyond Plants and Animals

The history of taxonomy is the history of discovering life that refuses to fit into simple boxes. While the ancient world saw only [[Plantae]] (fixed, photosynthesizing) and [[Animalia]] (mobile, consuming), the invention of the **microscope** shattered this duality.

### 1. The Disruption of the Two-Kingdom Model
The discovery of microorganisms created immediate paradoxes. Organisms were found that held characteristics of *both* kingdoms.

> [!EXAMPLE] The *Euglena* Paradox
> The **[[Euglena]]** is the classic example of classification failure:
> * **Plant traits:** Contains [[Chlorophyll]] for photosynthesis.
> * **Animal traits:** Possesses an **eyespot** and moves via a **[[Flagellum]]**.
> 
> *Result:* It could not be logically classified as strictly plant or animal.

### 2. The Great Divide: Prokaryote vs. Eukaryote
In the mid-20th century, a fundamental cellular distinction replaced the plant/animal divide as the primary separation of life.

| Feature               | [[Prokaryotes]] (Prokaryota)          | [[Eukaryotes]] (Eukaryota)         |
| :-------------------- | :------------------------------------ | :--------------------------------- |
| **Nucleus**           | **Absent** (No nuclear membrane)      | **Present** (Membrane-bound)       |
| **Genetic Material**  | Free-floating                         | Contained in Nucleus               |
| **Kingdoms Included** | [[Monera]] (Bacteria, Archaebacteria) | Protista, Fungi, Plantae, Animalia |

![[Pasted image 20260203124638.png|center|500]]
### 3. The Taxonomic "Problem Children"
Several groups present unique challenges to classification, sitting on the boundaries of living definitions or evolutionary lines.
#### A. [[Viruses]]: The Living/Non-Living Threshold
Viruses are the most difficult to classify. Many biologists deny they are "living" at all.
* **Characteristics:** Obligate parasites (no free-living forms).
* **Structure:** Simple protein coat + genetic material ([[DNA]] *or* [[RNA]], never both).
* **Reproduction:** Do not divide by fission. They inject material into a host and hijack its protein synthesis.
* **Origin Theories:**
    1.  Primitive forms (due to simple structure)?
    2.  "Escaped" genes that became parasitic?

![[Pasted image 20260203124818.png|center|450]]
#### B. [[Blue-green Algae]] (Cyanobacteria)
Traditionally studied by botanists, but biologically bacteria-like.
* **Status:** Prokaryotic (part of Monera).
* **Plant-like Behavior:** Photosynthesize like green plants (releasing oxygen).
* **Evolutionary Link:** Likely the group closest to the ancestor of eukaryotic plants.

![[Pasted image 20260203125036.png|center|550]]

#### C. [[Fungi]]
Historically lumped with plants, but biologically distinct.
* **Plant-like:** Rigid cell walls; grow in "fruit" structures (mushrooms).
* **Non-Plant:**
    * No Chlorophyll (heterotrophic).
    * Cell walls often made of **[[Chitin]]** (like insects), not cellulose.
    * Multi-nucleated "cells."

![[Pasted image 20260203125215.png|center|500]]

### 4. Classification Schemes
Biologists have proposed various multi-kingdom systems to account for these distinctions.
#### The Problem of Protista
The kingdom **Protista** was proposed for unicellular organisms.
* *Critique:* Some "unicellular" plants form complex colonies with specialized cells, making the line between "Protist" and "Plant" blurry.

#### The Four-Kingdom Variation
A specific alternative scheme that attempts to categorize distinct biological strategies:

| Kingdom         | Members                                                                               |
| :-------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **1. Virus**    | Viruses (Placed in their own kingdom in this scheme).                                 |
| **2. Monera**   | Bacteria, blue-green algae, [[Archaebacteria]], prochlorophytes.                      |
| **3. Plantae**  | Algae, slime molds, [[Fungi\|True Fungi]], mosses, ferns, conifers, flowering plants. |
| **4. Animalia** | Protozoans, sponges, worms, arthropods, mollusks, vertebrates.                        |



> [!NOTE]
> 
> Modern taxonomy often favors a **Five-Kingdom** (Whittaker) or **Three-Domain** system, but the relationships remain a subject of active scientific debate.

## A Classification of Living Organisms

Recent advances in **biochemistry**, **electron microscopy**, and **genetic homology** have solidified the support for a **Five-Kingdom Classification** system. This system redefines relationships based on internal structure and genetic relatedness rather than just morphology.

### 1. The Five Kingdoms: Comparative Overview
The living world is divided into one Prokaryotic kingdom and four Eukaryotic kingdoms.

| Feature | [[Monera]] | [[Protista]] | [[Fungi]] | [[Plantae]] | [[Animalia]] |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Cell Type** | **Prokaryotic** (No nucleus) | **Eukaryotic** | **Eukaryotic** | **Eukaryotic** | **Eukaryotic** |
| **Complexity** | Unicellular | Unicellular (mostly) | Multicellular (mostly) | Multicellular | Multicellular |
| **Nutrition** | Various | All modes | **Osmotrophic** (Absorptive) | **Autotrophic** (Photosynthesis) | **Heterotrophic** (Ingestive) |
| **Cell Wall** | [[Peptidoglycan]] | Various | [[Chitin]] | [[Cellulose]] | **Absent** |
| **Motility** | Bacterial Flagella | Flagella, Cilia, Pseudopodia | Non-motile | Non-motile | Motile (Muscular/Nervous) |

#### Key Biological Distinctions
* **Monera (The Prokaryotes):** Includes bacteria and [[Archaebacteria]]. They reproduce by fission and possess a unique cell wall component called *peptidoglycan* (crucial for Gram staining).

![[Pasted image 20260203130926.png|center|500]]

* **Protista (The Bridge):** A mix of plant-like and animal-like microscopic organisms.
    * *Naming Convention Issue:* Botanists use the term "Division" while Zoologists use "Phylum." Protista uses both: "Phylum" for protozoa (animal-like) and "Division" for algae (plant-like).
* **Plantae:** Defined by cell walls, photosynthesis, and a life cycle featuring **Alternation of Generations** (haploid gametophyte vs. diploid sporophyte).
* **Animalia:** Defined by the lack of cell walls and the development of complex **organ systems** due to independent motility.

> [!WARNING] The Difficulty of Classification: *Aschelminthes*
> Classification is rarely settled. For example, the **[[Aschelminthes]]** (pseudocoelomates) have been classified in three different ways:
> 1.  As one Phylum with five classes.
> 2.  Split into five separate Phyla.
> 3.  Regrouped into new relationships (e.g., Phylum Introverta).
>
> This highlights that true phylogenetic relationships for some groups remain to be established.
